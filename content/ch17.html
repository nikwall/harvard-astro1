17.1 The Big Bang theory

An expanding universe was smaller, hotter and denser in the past. Early on it was so hot that photons could turn into particle–antiparticle pairs (pair production, E = mc²), and pairs annihilated back into photons. The universe was a soup of radiation and elementary particles. Physics we have tested in particle accelerators takes us back to ~10−10 s; before that, the theory is more speculative.

Definitions
Four forces
Gravity, electromagnetism, the strong force (binds nuclei) and the weak force (radioactive decay). They're thought to merge at high energies (GUT, “theory of everything”).
Antimatter
Same mass, opposite charge (an antiproton is negative). Matter + antimatter → pure energy (photons).
Recombination
The moment (380,000 yr) when neutral atoms formed and the universe became transparent.

17.2 Evidence for the Big Bang

1. The cosmic microwave background (CMB). Predicted in the 1940s; found by accident in 1965 by Penzias and Wilson as a “noise” coming from every direction (Nobel 1978). The Big Bang predicts exactly what we see: thermal radiation (it came from a hot, opaque plasma), nearly the same in all directions, redshifted from 3000 K to a few K. COBE (1992), WMAP and Planck mapped tiny temperature ripples of ~1 part in 100,000: the density seeds of all later galaxies.

2. Helium and light-element abundances. The theory predicts that ~25% of ordinary mass became helium in the first minutes, and we measure ~25% everywhere, even in the oldest, least-enriched gas. Stars could have made only a few percent in 14 Gyr. Deuterium and lithium abundances also match, and they pin down the density of ordinary matter, which turns out to be far too little to account for dark matter (Ch. 18).

Plus: the expansion itself (Hubble's law) and an evolving universe (galaxies and quasars were different long ago). The rival steady-state model (the universe is infinitely old and unchanging) couldn't explain the CMB and died in the 1960s.

17.3–17.4 Inflation, and why the night is dark

Inflation: at ~10−38 s, as the strong force separated, the universe may have expanded by a factor of ~1030 in ~10−36 s. It solves three puzzles:

1 · StructureTiny quantum fluctuations were stretched to cosmic size and became the density ripples that grew into galaxies.
2 · SmoothnessOpposite sides of the sky are too far apart to have ever exchanged heat, yet the CMB is uniform to 10−5. Before inflation they were in contact.
3 · FlatnessSpace has “flat” geometry (total density = critical density). Inflating something enormously flattens it, like the surface of a hugely inflated balloon.

Did it happen? Inflation predicts the specific statistical pattern of the CMB ripples (their sizes and strengths), and the Planck data match it beautifully. Direct proof (primordial gravitational waves) is still being sought.

Olbers' paradox: if the universe were infinite, unchanging and filled with stars, every line of sight would end on a star and the night sky would blaze like the Sun's surface. It's dark because the universe has a finite age: we see only stars whose light has had time to reach us, and they haven't shone forever. So the dark night sky is evidence for a changing universe with a beginning.

Going further Deeper physics, history & more practice

Going deeper · a Big Bang you can calculate (Owocki §30, 32–33)
  1. Critical density. Treat a sphere of galaxies like a ball thrown up: it just escapes if kinetic = potential energy, ½(H0r)² = G(4π/3)ρr³/r. So ρc = 3H0²/(8πG) ≈ 9×10−27 kg/m³, about 5 hydrogen atoms per cubic metre.
  2. Ages. A coasting universe is 1/H0 = 14 Gyr old (H0 = 70). A critical matter-only universe decelerates, so it's only ⅔ of that, 9.3 Gyr, younger than the ~12 Gyr globular clusters. That “age crisis” was solved by dark energy (Ch. 18).
  3. Temperature history. Radiation cools as the universe stretches: T = 2.73 K × (1 + z). At z ≈ 1100, T ≈ 3000 K: hydrogen recombined and the universe became transparent. That glow peaked near 1 μm; stretched 1100×, today it peaks at 1.06 mm (microwaves).
  4. Why 25% helium? When nucleosynthesis began (~3 min), there was ~1 neutron per 7 protons. Nearly every neutron ended up in ⁴He (2n + 2p). Out of 16 nucleons (2 n, 14 p), 4 are in helium, so the helium mass fraction Y = 2(n/p)/(1 + n/p) = 2(1/7)/(8/7) = 0.25, as observed everywhere.
  5. Photons outnumber atoms ~2×109 : 1. That's the leftover of matter–antimatter annihilation: roughly one extra proton per billion survived. The ratio also controls how much deuterium survives, an independent measure of ordinary matter (~5% of critical).
History & method · from The Cosmos
Olbers's paradox: if the universe were infinite, eternal and unchanging, every line of sight would end on a star and the night would blaze; it's dark mainly because the universe has a finite age, so light from beyond ~14 Gly hasn't reached us. Steady state (Hoyle, Bondi, Gold, 1948): expansion with matter created continuously, so there's no beginning. It died with the CMB (Penzias & Wilson, 1965), which a steady state can't produce. COBE (1992) found the CMB is a perfect black body with 1-in-105 ripples (Nobel 2006, Mather & Smoot). The CMB is slightly hotter in one direction and cooler in the opposite: our own motion (~370 km/s).

recap
Run the expansion backward and the universe gets hotter and denser, down to a soup of radiation and particles. It passed through eras as forces separated, matter beat antimatter, helium formed (25%) in minutes, and at 380,000 years atoms formed, releasing the CMB (now 2.73 K, uniform to 10−5). The CMB and H/He abundances are the key confirmations. Inflation explains structure, smoothness and flatness. The dark night sky shows the universe has a finite age.
