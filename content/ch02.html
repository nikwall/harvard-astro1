2.1 Patterns in the night sky

Definitions
Celestial sphere
The imaginary sphere on which stars appear to lie. It's a 2D projection: stars in one constellation (one of 88 sky regions) can be at very different distances.
Celestial poles · celestial equator
Earth's poles and equator projected onto the sky. The ecliptic is the Sun's apparent yearly path, tilted 23½° to the celestial equator.
Horizon · zenith · meridian
The local sky: the horizon separates Earth and sky, the zenith is straight overhead, and the meridian runs N–zenith–S. A position is given by altitude (angle above the horizon) and direction (azimuth).
Circumpolar star
A star that circles the pole without ever setting.
Sidereal vs. solar day
One rotation relative to the stars takes 23 h 56 min; noon to noon takes 24 h. Earth's orbital motion adds ~4 min, so stars rise ~4 min earlier each night.

From anywhere on Earth we see half the celestial sphere as the dome of our local sky. Earth's rotation (west → east) makes every star appear to circle the celestial pole once a day, rising in the east and setting in the west. Stars close enough to the pole never set: they are circumpolar.

What you see depends on latitude and time of year, not on longitude. Latitude tilts your horizon relative to the celestial sphere. Beijing and Philadelphia (same latitude) see the same stars, just at different times. The time of year matters because the night side of Earth faces a different direction in space as we orbit, so the Sun “hides” different constellations of the zodiac through the year.

Key insight · rules of thumb for the sky
  • Altitude of the celestial pole = your latitude. Polaris stands 42° high in Cambridge and 0° at the equator.
  • The celestial equator crosses the meridian at altitude 90° − latitude.
  • In the N hemisphere a star is circumpolar if its declination is > 90° − latitude.
  • Hand at arm's length: fist ≈ 10°, finger ≈ 1°. The Sun and Moon are both ≈ ½°.
Toolkit · angles, sizes and distances
Angular size formula (small angles)$$\frac{\text{angular size}}{360^\circ} = \frac{\text{physical size}}{2\pi \times \text{distance}}$$Equivalently θ[rad] = s/d, θ[″] = 206,265 × s/d. Units: 1° = 60′ (arcmin), 1′ = 60″ (arcsec); a full circle = 21,600′ = 1,296,000″.
Sun's altitude at noon (sundial rule)$$h_{\text{noon}} = 90^\circ - \text{latitude} + \delta_\odot$$δ☉ = Sun's declination: +23.5° (June solstice), 0° (equinoxes), −23.5° (Dec. solstice).
Worked: the Moon spans 0.5° at 380,000 km, so its size is 0.5° × 2π × 380,000 km ÷ 360° ≈ 3300 km (true value 3476 km). The Sun spans 0.5° at 1.5×108 km, giving ≈ 1.3×106 km. Same angular size, 400× bigger and 400× farther: that coincidence makes total solar eclipses possible.

2.2 The reason for seasons

Earth's axis is tilted 23½° and keeps pointing toward Polaris. In June the Northern Hemisphere is tipped toward the Sun, and two things happen:

  • More direct sunlight: the Sun is higher, so each square meter of ground gets more energy. Low winter sunlight spreads over more area.
  • Longer days: more hours of heating than of cooling.

Distance is not the cause: Earth's distance varies only ~3%, and we're actually closest to the Sun in early January. The hemispheres have opposite seasons at the same time.

Solstices and equinoxes. June solstice (~June 21): the Sun is farthest north, giving the longest day in the N. At the equinoxes (~March 21, ~Sept. 22) the Sun is on the celestial equator: 12 h of day everywhere, and it rises due east and sets due west. December solstice (~Dec. 21): the Sun is farthest south. The Sun is overhead only in the tropics (±23.5°), and there are 24 h days or nights beyond the Arctic and Antarctic circles (66.5°).

Precession: the axis wobbles like a top in a 26,000-year cycle. The tilt stays 23½°, but the pole star changes: Thuban ~5000 years ago, Vega in ~12,000 years. It also drifts the zodiac, so astrological “Sun signs” are now off by about a month.

2.3 The Moon: phases and eclipses

Phases are geometry, not shadows. The Moon's cycle takes 29½ days (synodic month; the orbit relative to the stars takes 27.3 days). New moon: the Moon is between Earth and Sun. Full moon: it's opposite the Sun, so it rises at sunset. Because the phase fixes the angle from the Sun, it also fixes the rise time:

First quarternoon6 pmmidnight
Full6 pmmidnight6 am
Third quartermidnight6 amnoon

Synchronous rotation: the Moon spins once per orbit (27.3 days), so it always shows us the same face. The far side is not permanently dark: it gets two weeks of daylight too.

Eclipses need two conditions: (1) the right phase (new for a solar eclipse, full for a lunar eclipse) and (2) the Moon crossing the ecliptic (a node), because its orbit is tilted ~5°. That's why we don't get eclipses every month; they cluster in two eclipse seasons per year. A lunar eclipse (total, partial or penumbral) is visible from Earth's whole night side. A total solar eclipse is seen only along a narrow path under the Moon's umbra, and it becomes annular when the Moon is near its farthest point and looks slightly smaller than the Sun. Eclipse patterns repeat every saros cycle of 18 yr 11⅓ d.

2.4 The ancient mystery of the planets

Planets usually drift eastward against the stars, but sometimes loop backward for weeks: apparent retrograde motion. In a Sun-centered view this is simple: Earth overtakes an outer planet (like a faster car passing a slower one), which then seems to slip backward. The Greeks mostly rejected a moving Earth because they saw no stellar parallax, the small annual shift of nearby stars. It exists, but even the largest (Proxima Centauri) is only 0.77″, far too small for the naked eye. The stars are simply far away.

Myth vs. reality
✗ Seasons come from the Earth–Sun distance. ✓ They come from the tilt; the hemispheres have opposite seasons, and we're closest in January.
✗ Phases are Earth's shadow. ✓ Earth's shadow falls on the Moon only in a lunar eclipse; phases are viewing geometry.
✗ Polaris is the brightest star. ✓ It's ~50th; it's special because it sits near the celestial pole.
✗ The Sun is overhead at noon. ✓ Only in the tropics. From Boston it never reaches the zenith.

Practice Exam-style questions with worked answers

Quick QuizTwo stars in the same constellation must be…?
Nothing in particular: they only lie in the same direction. They may be very far apart in space.
Quick QuizThe north celestial pole is 35° above your horizon. Where are you?
Latitude 35°N (pole altitude = latitude). Longitude can't be read from the pole.
Quick QuizIn winter Earth's axis points at Polaris. In spring it points at…?
Polaris still. The axis direction is fixed on human timescales; only precession (26,000 yr) changes it.
Quick QuizIf the Sun rises precisely due east, then…?
It must be an equinox (March or September), when the Sun is on the celestial equator. This works at any latitude.
Quick QuizOne week after full moon the phase is…? When does it rise?
Third quarter (a quarter cycle later). It rises around midnight and is highest at dawn.
Quick QuizA total lunar eclipse tonight tells you the Moon is…?
Full. Only then is it behind Earth, opposite the Sun, where Earth's shadow can fall on it.
Quick QuizSaturn is in apparent retrograde motion. What's happening?
Earth is passing Saturn, with both planets on the same side of the Sun. Saturn never actually moves backward.
ProblemMoon: 3476 km, 356,400–406,700 km away. Sun: 1.39×106 km, 1.475–1.526×108 km away. Total eclipse possible when both are farthest?
θ = s/d × 57.3°: Moon 0.56°–0.49°; Sun 0.54°–0.52°. Both farthest: Moon 0.49° < Sun 0.52°, so it can't cover the Sun. No total eclipse, only annular.
ProblemHow many arcseconds in a circle? What's ½° in ′ and ″?
360 × 60 × 60 = 1.3×106″ (21,600′). ½° = 30′ = 1800″.

Going further Activities, deeper physics & more practice

Hirshfeld Activity 1 · Aristarchus measures the Sun with the half Moon (c. 250 BCE)
At first or last quarter the Moon is exactly half lit, so the Sun–Moon–Earth angle is 90°. Measure the Moon–Sun angle E seen from Earth and the right triangle gives the Sun's distance d in Moon distances r: d = r / cos E.
  1. Aristarchus's E = 87°: d = r/0.052 = 19 r. With 88°: 29 r. The true 89.85°: ~380 r. The answer is extremely sensitive to E.
  2. During a total solar eclipse the Moon just covers the Sun, so the same angular size means the Sun is 19 Moon diameters across (by his numbers). With the Moon 1/3 of Earth (from lunar-eclipse shadows): 6.3 Earth diameters.
  3. Sector formula r = 57.3 s/θ with θ = ½°: r = 57.3 × 6.3/0.5 ≈ 730 Earth diameters. The truth: 109 across, 11,700 away. The method was right; the angle was impossible to measure without telescopes. Still, it put the Sun far bigger than Earth, and Aristarchus proposed a Sun-centred universe.
Going deeper · solid angle, and why the Sun and Moon look the same size (Owocki §2)
Angles on the sky are 2D: a patch of angular radius α covers a solid angle Ω ≈ πα². The whole sky is 4π steradians = 41,253 square degrees. The Sun and Moon (α ≈ ¼°) each cover ~0.2 deg², 1/200,000 of the sky. Their near-equal size is a coincidence: the Sun is ~400× wider and ~400× farther. Because the Moon slowly recedes (Ch. 4), total eclipses won't last forever; annular ones already happen when the Moon is near apogee.
From The Cosmos · sky facts that show up in exams
Stars twinkle, planets don't: a star is a point, bent by turbulent air; a planet is a tiny disk whose many points average out. Total lunar eclipse = copper-red: sunlight refracted through Earth's atmosphere (all the world's sunsets) reaches the Moon. Eclipses need nodes: the Moon's orbit is tilted ~5°, so eclipses happen only in two “seasons” a year. Gregorian rule: leap year every 4 years, except century years not divisible by 400 (1900 wasn't, 2000 was). Precession (26,000 yr) makes Vega the pole star around 14,000 CE.
CosmosHow many times brighter is a 3rd-magnitude star than a 10th-magnitude star?
7 magnitudes: 100.4×7 = 2.57 ≈ 630×. (5 magnitudes = exactly 100×.)
OwockiA 20 cm party balloon floats 1 m above you. Angular size? How high must it float to match the Moon?
0.2 rad = 11°. For ½° (0.0087 rad): d = 0.2/0.0087 ≈ 23 m.
HirshfeldSuppose the Sun were only 3× farther than the Moon but looked the same size. How big would it be?
Same angle, 3× the distance → 3 Moon diameters (s = rθ). Size and distance trade one for one.

30-second
recap
The sky is a projected sphere measured in angles. Rotation makes stars rise and set, and the pole's altitude equals your latitude. The 23½° tilt (not distance!) makes seasons by changing sunlight angle and day length; precession slowly swings the axis. The Moon's phase is Sun–Earth–Moon geometry; eclipses need new/full moon and a node crossing. Retrograde loops are Earth overtaking planets, and invisible parallax fooled the Greeks.
