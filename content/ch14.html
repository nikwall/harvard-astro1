14.1 White dwarfs

A white dwarf is the exposed core of a low-mass star: about the Sun's mass packed into roughly Earth's size (a teaspoon weighs several tons), held up by electron degeneracy pressure. Surprisingly, more massive white dwarfs are smaller: extra gravity squeezes the electrons into less space. Chandrasekhar (age 19, 1931) showed there's a maximum, the white dwarf limit of 1.4 M☉, above which the electrons would have to move faster than light, so collapse is inevitable.

White dwarfs in close binaries: gas from a companion spirals in through a hot accretion disk (conservation of angular momentum again). Hydrogen piles up on the surface until it ignites in a burst of fusion: a nova (up to 100,000 L☉ for weeks; it can repeat). If accretion or a merger of two white dwarfs pushes the mass to the 1.4 M☉ limit, carbon fusion runs away through the whole star: a white dwarf supernova (“Type Ia”). The star is completely destroyed, nothing is left, and it outshines a massive-star supernova. Their spectra lack hydrogen lines. Because they all explode near the same mass, they're standard candles (Ch. 16), and they make much of the universe's iron.

14.2 Neutron stars

A neutron star is the collapsed iron core of a massive star: 1.4–2 M☉ in a ball ~20 km across, essentially one giant atomic nucleus supported by neutron degeneracy pressure. A paperclip of it would outweigh Mount Everest. Conservation of angular momentum makes newborn neutron stars spin up to many times per second, and they have magnetic fields up to 1012× Earth's.

Pulsars: in 1967 graduate student Jocelyn Bell found radio pulses repeating every 1.3 s. The explanation is a rotating neutron star whose magnetic poles beam radiation that sweeps past Earth like a lighthouse. Only a compact neutron star can spin that fast without flying apart. The Crab pulsar, left by the supernova Chinese astronomers saw in 1054, flashes 30 times per second and is slowly winding down. In X-ray binaries, gas falling onto a neutron star heats to millions of degrees and glows in X rays; helium igniting on the surface makes X-ray bursts. The maximum neutron star mass is ~2–3 M☉; above it, nothing known can stop collapse.

14.3 Black holes: gravity's ultimate victory

Relativity in brief
Special relativity (1905)
Everyone measures the same speed of light; nothing travels faster. Moving clocks run slow and moving lengths contract.
General relativity (1915)
Gravity is the curvature of spacetime by mass. The equivalence principle: gravity and acceleration are indistinguishable locally. Clocks run slow in strong gravity, and light climbing out is gravitationally redshifted.
Event horizon
The boundary from inside which not even light escapes. Its radius is the Schwarzschild radius.
Singularity
The center, where known physics breaks down.

A black hole is where gravity has crushed matter so far that the escape velocity exceeds c: a hole in observable spacetime. For a stellar black hole the event horizon is only tens of km across.

A visit: from a safe distance you could simply orbit it like any other mass (black holes don't “suck”). Watching a friend fall in, you'd see their clock slow down and their light redshift ever more, and they would fade from view without ever quite crossing. The friend feels nothing special at the horizon, except the tidal forces that stretch (“spaghettify”) them. Those tidal forces are deadly near small black holes but gentle at the horizon of a supermassive one.

Do they exist? Yes. (1) X-ray binaries whose unseen companion is too massive to be a neutron star (Cygnus X-1: ~21 M☉). (2) Gravitational waves from merging black holes (LIGO, 2015 onward; hundreds of events so far). (3) Stars racing around the Milky Way's center (Ch. 15) and the Event Horizon Telescope images of the black-hole “shadows” in M87 (2019) and Sagittarius A* (2022).

14.4 Extreme events

Gamma-ray bursts come from extremely distant galaxies and are the most powerful explosions known. Long bursts come from especially powerful supernovae that form black holes; short bursts from neutron-star mergers. Gravitational waves, predicted by Einstein, are ripples in spacetime. Orbiting compact pairs radiate them, lose energy and spiral together. In 2017 (GW170817) two neutron stars merged; telescopes worldwide saw the flash (a kilonova) and detected freshly made heavy elements, confirming that such mergers forge much of the universe's gold, platinum and uranium.

Practice Exam-style questions with worked answers

Quick QuizSmallest: 1.2 M☉ white dwarf, 0.6 M☉ white dwarf, or Jupiter? Largest: 1.2 M☉ WD, 1.5 M☉ NS, or 3 M☉ BH?
Smallest: the 1.2 M☉ white dwarf (more mass = smaller). Largest: that same white dwarf (Earth-size vs. ~10 km vs. RS = 9 km).
Quick QuizSeeing a nova means…? What if the Sun became a same-mass black hole?
A white dwarf in a binary (surface H fusion). Earth's orbit wouldn't change, though it would get dark and cold.
Quick QuizWhy do neutron-star pairs merge? Which system most likely holds a black hole?
Gravitational waves carry away orbital energy. A black hole is likeliest in the X-ray binary with an O star and an equal-mass (>3 M☉) partner.
Quick QuizA flashing red light falling into a black hole appears…? Weakest tidal forces at the horizon?
Its flashes slow down and shift to the infrared. The weakest tides: the 106 M☉ black hole (its horizon is far from the center).
Quick QuizAn accretion disk orbiting opposite to a neutron star's spin will…?
Slow its spin: the infalling gas carries angular momentum in the opposite sense.
ProblemCrab pulsar: p = 0.0333 s, slowing by 4.2×10−13 s per s. Age ≈ p/(2r)?
0.0333/(8.4×10−13) ≈ 4.0×1010 s ≈ 1250 yr, close to the ~970 years since the 1054 supernova.
ProblemWhat black-hole mass has an average density (inside RS) equal to water's?
ρ = M/(4/3 πRS³) ∝ 1/M², so M = √(3c⁶/(32πG³ρ)) ≈ 2.7×1038 kg ≈ 108 M☉. Big black holes aren't dense.
ProblemSchwarzschild radius of a 5 M☉ black hole? Of you (70 kg)?
5 × 3 km = 15 km. You: 70 kg = 3.5×10−29 M☉, so RS ≈ 10−25 m, 10−10 of a proton.
ProblemEnergy radiated when 29 + 36 M☉ merge into 62 M☉?
Δm = 3 M☉ = 6×1030 kg, so E = mc² ≈ 5×1047 J, with a peak power briefly exceeding the light output of all stars in the observable universe combined.

Hirshfeld Activities 17–19 · the Crab's age, event horizons, and weighing black holes
  1. Crab Nebula (Act. 17): compare photos from 1950 and 2000. Each knot's angular distance from the pulsar grew; age T = (angular position)/(angular speed) per knot, averaged: ~950 yr before 2000, i.e. an explosion around 1050, consistent with the supernova seen in 1054. Size: 1500 km/s × 3.0×1010 s = 4.5×1013 km radius → ~9.5 ly across. With an angular diameter of ~5–6′ (~330″): d = 206,265 × 9.5 ly/330″ ≈ 6000 ly (accepted ~6500 ly).
  2. Escape speeds (Act. 18): vesc = 11 km/s × √(M/R) in Earth units. White dwarf (1 M☉, Earth-sized): ~6000 km/s; neutron star (1 M☉, ~10 km): 0.5–0.8 c. Michell (1783): a Sun-density star 500× the Sun's radius has vesc = 618 km/s × 500 ≈ c, the first “dark star”. Event horizon RS = 2GM/c² = 3 km per M☉; Earth's would be 9 mm (a marble).
  3. Kepler 3 for black holes (Act. 19): M = a³/P² (M☉, AU, yr). Cygnus X-1: 0.2 AU, 5.6 d = 0.0153 yr → 34 M☉ total; minus the 20 M☉ supergiant → ~14 M☉, above any neutron star's maximum (~2–3 M☉), so a black hole. GW150914: 350 km apart, P = 0.0133 s → ~70 M☉ (LIGO: 36 + 29); both must be smaller than 175 km, and neutron stars can't be that heavy, so they're black holes (RS of 30 M☉ = 90 km). A 10 M☉ black hole in the Sun's place: Earth's year would be √(1/10) yr = 115 days.
Going deeper · the remnant limits and the most efficient engine in nature (Owocki §19–20)
White dwarfs: electron degeneracy pressure makes R ∝ M−1/3: heavier white dwarfs are smaller. As M grows the electrons approach c, the radius drops out of the balance, and a maximum mass appears: the Chandrasekhar limit, 1.4 M☉. The same argument with neutrons gives the neutron-star limit, ~2–2.3 M☉. Accretion: matter spiralling to a few RS radiates η ≈ 10–40% of mc², vs. 0.7% for fusion; the disk's inner edge reaches ~107 K and glows in X-rays. Heavy elements: the 2017 neutron-star merger (GW170817) showed a kilonova: much of the universe's gold and platinum is made in such mergers.
CosmosSchwarzschild radius of a 10 M☉ black hole? Radius of its photon sphere?
RS = 3 km × 10 = 30 km; photon sphere at 1.5 RS = 45 km (light can orbit there).
CosmosType II vs. Type Ia supernova: how do you tell them apart, and what's left?
II: hydrogen lines, core collapse of a massive star → neutron star or black hole. Ia: no hydrogen, a white dwarf blown apart at 1.4 M☉ → nothing left. (Ib/Ic: stripped massive stars, also core collapse.)
OwockiEnergy released when 1 kg falls into a black hole's disk at η = 0.1, vs. burning 1 kg of hydrogen by fusion?
0.1 × mc² = 9×1015 J; fusion gives 0.007 mc² = 6×1014 J: accretion is ~14× better.

30-second
recap
Stellar corpses come in three kinds. White dwarfs (Earth-size, electron degeneracy, max 1.4 M☉) make novae and, at the limit, white-dwarf supernovae (standard candles, iron). Neutron stars (~20 km, neutron degeneracy) appear as pulsars and X-ray binaries. Above ~2–3 M☉ nothing stops collapse: a black hole, whose event horizon is RS ≈ 3 km × M/M☉. General relativity: gravity curves spacetime, slows time and redshifts light. Evidence: X-ray binaries, gravitational waves, EHT images. Neutron-star mergers make gold.
