15.1 The Milky Way revealed

The Milky Way is a spiral galaxy with a thin disk ~100,000 ly across (with spiral arms, gas and dust), a central bulge, and a roughly spherical halo of old stars and ~150 globular clusters. The Sun sits in the disk ~27,000 ly from the center. It holds a few hundred billion stars.

How we mapped it: Galileo resolved the band into stars. The Herschels counted stars in every direction and thought we were near the center, because dust hid the rest. In 1920 Harlow Shapley (later director of the Harvard College Observatory) used the distribution of globular clusters to show that the center lies far away in Sagittarius. Radio (21-cm hydrogen) and infrared maps later revealed the spiral structure.

Orbits: disk stars all orbit the center in the same direction and nearly the same plane, bobbing gently up and down through it (the disk's gravity pulls them back). Halo stars (and some bulge stars) orbit at random inclinations, plunging through the disk. The Sun orbits at ~220 km/s, once every ~230 Myr.

Weighing the galaxy: an orbit's speed tells us the mass inside it. The Sun's orbit gives ~1011 M☉ within 27,000 ly. Stars and gas farther out orbit just as fast, revealing a massive, invisible dark matter halo (total ~1012 M☉, Ch. 18).

Toolkit · the orbital velocity formula
$$M_r = \frac{r\,v^2}{G}$$The mass enclosed within a circular orbit of radius r and speed v (Newton's laws). A faster orbit at the same r means more mass inside.
Worked: Sun: r = 27,000 ly = 2.6×1020 m, v = 2.2×105 m/s, so M = (2.6×1020)(2.2×105)²/6.67×10−11 ≈ 1.9×1041 kg ≈ 1011 M☉. Check it on Saturn's rings: r = 67,000 km, v = 23.8 km/s gives 5.7×1026 kg, which is Saturn's mass.

15.2 Galactic recycling: the star–gas–star cycle

The interstellar medium (ISM) is gas (by mass ~70% H, 28% He, 2% heavier elements) plus dust. The galaxy constantly recycles it:

Stars return gasvia winds, planetary nebulae and supernovae, enriched with new elements
Hot bubblessupernova blast waves heat gas to millions of K (seen in X rays)
Atomic H cloudsgas cools to warm atomic hydrogen (mapped by its 21-cm radio line)
Molecular cloudscools further to 10–30 K, forms H₂ and CO (seen in radio CO lines)
New starsgravity collapses cloud cores and the cycle repeats

Each cycle adds heavy elements. Stars forming near the Sun today have ~2% heavy elements, while the oldest halo stars have as little as 0.01%. The cycle isn't perfectly closed: some gas stays locked in low-mass stars and remnants, and fresh gas falls in from intergalactic space.

Where stars form: mostly in the spiral arms. Arms are density waves, like a traffic jam that cars pass through: gas clouds are compressed as they pass through, triggering star formation. Short-lived blue O and B stars never travel far from the arm where they were born, which makes arms look bright and blue. Their UV light ionizes surrounding hydrogen into glowing red ionization nebulae (like the Orion Nebula). Dust scatters blue light into reflection nebulae, and dense clouds block light as dark nebulae.

Course link · L18: the high-energy sky and cosmic rays
Cosmic rays are charged particles (mostly protons, some nuclei and electrons) moving close to the speed of light, filling the galaxy. The main accelerators are supernova shock waves: particles bounce back and forth across the shock and gain energy each crossing (“diffusive shock acceleration”). Our magnetic field and atmosphere shield us. When cosmic rays hit carbon and oxygen nuclei in space they split them, which is how most lithium, beryllium and boron were made (the pink tiles in Ch. 14's periodic table). The highest-energy ones likely come from outside the galaxy, from active galactic nuclei.

15.3–15.4 History of the Milky Way and its central black hole

Two stellar populations: disk population stars are young to old and rich in heavy elements, in orderly orbits. Halo population stars are all old, low-mass (the massive ones died long ago) and metal-poor, in random orbits. So the halo stars formed first, before the gas had been enriched and before it settled into a disk.

Formation: several protogalactic clouds of H and He merged. Early stars formed in them with random orbits and became the halo. The remaining gas collapsed and, conserving angular momentum, spun into a flat disk, where stars have formed ever since. The halo no longer forms stars (its gas is gone). Gaia found the debris of a big early merger (“Gaia–Enceladus”, ~10 Gyr ago), and small galaxies such as the Sagittarius dwarf are being absorbed today.

The galactic center: the compact radio source Sgr A*. Stars there move on tight, fast orbits (S2 reaches ~3% of light speed at closest approach), which reveal a mass of ~4 million M☉ in a tiny volume: only a supermassive black hole fits (Nobel Prize 2020: Genzel and Ghez). The Event Horizon Telescope imaged its shadow in 2022. It's quiet today, unlike the quasars of Ch. 16.

Myth vs. reality
✗ The halo is a ring (like an angel's halo). ✓ It's a roughly spherical region surrounding the disk.
✗ Explosions in space are loud. ✓ Interstellar gas is far too thin to carry sound to your ears.

Practice Exam-style questions with worked answers

Quick QuizHalo shape? Where are most globular clusters?
The halo is round like a ball, and most globular clusters are in the halo.
Quick QuizWhy do disk stars bob up and down?
The gravity of the disk pulls them back each time they rise above or below it: an oscillation, not friction.
Quick QuizHow do we find the mass outside the Sun's orbit?
From orbits of stars and gas clouds farther out than the Sun. Each orbit weighs only what's inside it.
Quick QuizColdest gas? Heavy-element share of stars forming now? Most penetrating light through the disk?
The disk (molecular clouds); ~2%; infrared (and radio): dust blocks visible light, blue most of all.
Quick QuizIonization nebula: halo, bulge or disk? Most likely halo star: O, A or M?
In the disk (young massive stars in arms). In the halo: an M star, because only old, long-lived low-mass stars remain.
ProblemA star orbits the center at 1000 km/s at a radius of 20 light-days. Central mass?
r = 20 × 2.6×1013 m = 5.2×1014 m, so M = rv²/G = 5.2×1014×1012/6.67×10−11 ≈ 7.8×1036 kg ≈ 4×106 M☉.
ProblemThe LMC orbits at 160,000 ly and ~300 km/s. Mass within?
r = 1.5×1021 m, v² = 9×1010, so M ≈ 2×1042 kg ≈ 1012 M☉, 10× more than inside the Sun's orbit: dark matter.
ProblemA globular cluster: stars at 50 ly orbit at 10 km/s. Mass?
r = 4.7×1017 m, so M = 4.7×1017 × 108/6.67×10−11 ≈ 7×1035 kg ≈ 3.5×105 M☉.
Quick QuizBest measurement of the central black hole's mass?
The orbits of stars around it (Kepler/Newton). Gas can be pushed by non-gravitational forces, and brightness doesn't give mass.

Going further Activities, deeper physics & more practice

Hirshfeld Activity 20 · Shapley's globular clusters find the galactic centre (1919)
Globular clusters mostly lie above and below the dusty disk, so we can see them far away, and a third of them crowd into Sagittarius (2% of the sky). Plot each cluster's X (toward the centre) and Z (above/below the plane) coordinates; the centre of the swarm marks the galactic centre.
  1. The swarm centres ~25,000–30,000 ly from the Sun (the average X of the 20 clusters), roughly halfway out in a disk of radius ~50,000 ly and ~2000 ly thick: we live in the suburbs, not the middle.
  2. Orbital speed: 2π × 26,000 ly × 9.5×1012 km/ly ÷ (2×108 yr × 8766 h/yr) ≈ 9×105 km/h ≈ 250 km/s.
  3. Shapley was right about the off-centre Sun but overestimated the size (~300,000 ly): he didn't correct for interstellar dust, which makes distant stars look fainter and so farther. Trumpler proved the dimming in 1930 using star clusters.
Activity 19 bonus: a star at the centre orbits in 15 yr at 1000 AU (like S2): M = 1000³/15² = 4.4×106 M☉, the supermassive black hole Sgr A*.
Going deeper · mapping the Galaxy by radio and weighing it by rotation (Owocki §12, 21, 26)
Toward the galactic centre, dust dims visible light by ~25 magnitudes, a factor 1010, but infrared and radio pass through. The 21-cm line (hydrogen's spin flip, detected in 1951 by Ewen and Purcell at Harvard) maps the disk: along each line of sight, the largest Doppler shift comes from the cloud where the sight line is tangent to its orbit, giving the rotation speed at that radius. The flat rotation curve (~220 km/s out to 15+ kpc) means M(R) = v²R/G grows in proportion to R where there is little light: dark matter. The ISM comes in phases at roughly the same pressure: cold clouds (10–100 K, most of the mass), warm gas (~104 K), and hot supernova bubbles (106 K, most of the volume). HII regions glow red in Hα; reflection nebulae look blue (scattered starlight, as in the Pleiades).
CosmosA hydrogen cloud's 21-cm line (rest 21.0000 cm) is seen at 21.0021 cm. Motion?
Δλ/λ = 10−4 → 30 km/s away from us.
CosmosMass inside 10,000 ly of the centre if stars there orbit at 200 km/s?
M = v²r/G = (2×105)² × 9.5×1019 / 6.7×10−11 ≈ 6×1040 kg ≈ 3×1010 M☉.
OwockiAt the galactic centre (8 kpc away), how many AU does 1″ correspond to?
s = θd: 1″ × 8000 pc = 8000 AU (1″ at 1 pc = 1 AU). S2's orbit spans ~0.1″.

30-second
recap
The Milky Way is a disk (100,000 ly, orderly orbits, gas, star formation in spiral density waves) plus a bulge and a spherical halo of old, metal-poor stars and globular clusters. Orbital speeds give the mass (M = rv²/G), and outer orbits reveal dark matter. The star–gas–star cycle (stars → hot bubbles → atomic H → molecular clouds → stars) steadily enriches the gas to ~2% heavy elements. Halo stars formed first, before the gas collapsed into the disk. At the center: a 4-million-M☉ black hole, weighed with star orbits.
