Big question: What single set of physical laws governs apples, tides, planets and galaxies?

Coming in with

Kepler's laws (Ch. 3): how planets move. What was missing was why.

This chapter

Newton's three laws, the conservation laws (momentum, angular momentum, energy) and universal gravitation. Together they explain orbits, escape and tides, and let us weigh objects in space.

Leads to

Almost everything. Angular momentum → spinning disks that form planets (Ch. 6). Gravity + energy → stars (Ch. 11–13). Newton's Kepler 3 → exoplanets (10), binary stars (12), galaxy masses and dark matter (15, 18).

4.1–4.2 Describing motion and Newton's laws

Definitions
Speed · velocity
Speed is the rate of motion (m/s); velocity is speed plus direction.
Acceleration
Any change in velocity, in speed or direction. Falling objects near Earth accelerate at g ≈ 9.8 m/s².
Momentum · force
Momentum = mass × velocity. A (net) force is anything that changes momentum.
Mass vs. weight
Mass is the amount of matter (the same everywhere). Weight is the force that a scale measures, and it depends on gravity and acceleration.
Free-fall · weightlessness
Falling with nothing pushing back. Astronauts in orbit are weightless because they are constantly falling around Earth, not because gravity is absent.

Newton (Principia, 1687) showed that the same physical laws work on Earth and in the heavens. That single idea is why we can study the universe using physics learned in the lab.

  1. First law (inertia): an object moves at constant velocity unless a net force acts on it.
  2. Second law: \(F = ma\). Force = mass × acceleration (or rate of change of momentum).
  3. Third law: for every force there's an equal and opposite reaction force. A rocket pushes gas backward and the gas pushes the rocket forward, with no ground needed. To turn left, you fire gas to the right.

An object moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating because its direction changes. That's what gravity does to the Moon every second.

4.3 Conservation laws: what keeps planets going?

Conservation of angular momentum (angular momentum = m × v × r): with no external twisting force (torque), it stays constant. This explains why planets keep orbiting and spinning for billions of years, and it is Kepler's 2nd law: when r shrinks near perihelion, v must grow. A skater pulling in her arms spins faster; a collapsing gas cloud spins up and flattens into a disk (key for Ch. 6 and 13).

Conservation of energy: energy can't be created or destroyed, only transferred or transformed. Three basic kinds:

Kineticenergy of motion, ½mv². Thermal energy is the random kinetic energy of many particles; temperature measures its average (Kelvin: 0 K = absolute zero).
Radiativeenergy carried by light.
Potentialstored energy: gravitational (higher, or more spread out, means more), chemical, and mass-energy E = mc², the power source of stars (Ch. 11).

A shrinking interstellar cloud converts gravitational potential energy into thermal energy (it heats up) and then into radiation. That's how stars are born (Ch. 13).

4.4 The force of gravity

Universal law of gravitation: every mass attracts every other mass with a force proportional to the product of the masses and falling off with the square of the distance. Double the distance → ¼ the force; triple → 1/9.

Newton extended Kepler: (1) the laws apply to all orbiting objects (moons, binary stars, stars around galaxies); (2) orbits can be bound (ellipses) or unbound (parabolas, hyperbolas); (3) his version of Kepler 3 contains the masses, so measuring an orbit weighs the central object.

Orbital energy (kinetic + gravitational potential) stays constant unless it's exchanged with something else (another object's gravity, atmospheric drag, a rocket burn). With enough energy an object reaches escape velocity: about 11 km/s (40,000 km/h) from Earth's surface. Escape velocity doesn't depend on the escaping object's mass.

Tides: the Moon pulls harder on Earth's near side than on its center, and harder on the center than on the far side. This difference in gravity (the tidal force) stretches Earth along the Earth–Moon line, making two bulges. Earth rotates through both each day, so there are two high and two low tides daily. The Sun pulls on Earth ~180× harder than the Moon, yet its tidal effect is only about half the Moon's, because tidal force falls off steeply with distance (∝ M/d³).

Tidal friction: Earth's rotation drags the bulges slightly ahead of the Moon. The Moon's pull on them slows Earth's spin (days lengthen by ~2 ms per century), and the bulges' pull speeds the Moon up so it spirals outward (~4 cm/yr). The same process long ago locked the Moon into synchronous rotation (Ch. 2). Tidal forces also heat Io (Ch. 8) and shred stars near black holes (Ch. 14).

Myth vs. reality
✗ There's no gravity in space. ✓ At the ISS gravity is ~90% of surface strength. Astronauts float because they're in free-fall.
✗ Rockets push off the ground. ✓ Newton's 3rd law: expelled gas pushes back on the rocket, and that works in vacuum too.
✗ The Moon pulls the ocean into one bulge. ✓ Tides come from the difference in pull across Earth: two bulges, two high tides a day.
✗ A black hole would suck Earth in. ✓ A same-mass black hole at the Sun's place leaves Earth's orbit unchanged; gravity depends only on mass and distance.

Practice Exam-style questions with worked answers

Quick QuizWhich car is accelerating: 50 mph straight, 30 mph straight uphill, or 100 mph around a circular track?
The circular track: its direction keeps changing. Constant speed in a straight line means zero acceleration.
Quick QuizWho is weightless: a child in the air on a trampoline, a scuba diver, or an astronaut on the Moon?
The child in mid-air: she's in free-fall. The diver (buoyancy) and the astronaut on the Moon (1/6 g) both still have weight.
Quick QuizEarth's angular momentum at perihelion compared with aphelion?
The same, since it's conserved. Smaller r is exactly compensated by larger v (Kepler 2).
Quick QuizIf Earth were twice as far from the Sun, the gravitational force would be…?
¼ as strong (inverse square: 1/2² = 1/4).
Quick QuizA contracting interstellar cloud's gravitational potential energy…?
Gradually turns into other forms: thermal energy (the cloud heats up) and then radiation.
Quick QuizIf the Moon were closer to Earth, high tides would be…?
Higher: a closer Moon means a bigger difference in pull across Earth. Still two a day.
ProblemSame planet at 1 AU, but around a star of 4 M☉. Period?
p² ∝ a³/M, so p = 1 yr × √(1/4) = ½ year. (Doubling the planet's mass changes almost nothing.)
ProblemWeigh Earth: the Moon orbits in 27.3 d at 384,000 km.
M = 4π²(3.84×108)³ / [G (2.36×106 s)²] ≈ 6.0×1024 kg (Earth + Moon). Subtract the Moon (1/80): M⊕ ≈ 5.9×1024 kg.
ProblemWeigh the Milky Way: the Sun orbits in 230 Myr at 27,000 ly.
a = 2.6×1020 m, p = 7.3×1015 s, so M ≈ 4π²a³/(Gp²) ≈ 1.9×1041 kg ≈ 1011 M☉. That's only the mass inside the Sun's orbit.

Homework · HW 3 #2 “Tides”, worked
Constants: G = 6.67×10−11, M☉ = 1.99×1030 kg, d = 1.50×1011 m, R⊕ = 6.37×106 m.
  1. Accelerations. Centre: a = GM/d² = 5.93×10−3 m/s². Near side: GM/(d−R)² = 5.9319×10−3; far side: GM/(d+R)² = 5.9309×10−3 m/s². At two significant digits all three are 5.9×10−3: keep 5–6 digits, or the difference vanishes in rounding.
  2. Differences: near − centre = 5.05×10−7, centre − far = 5.05×10−7 m/s². Essentially equal (they differ only at the 10−5 level) because R ≪ d: Earth is stretched symmetrically, so there are two equal bulges.
  3. Approximation: 2GMR/d³ = 2(6.67×10−11)(1.99×1030)(6.37×106)/(1.50×1011)³ = 5.1×10−7 m/s². Same number; it's the first-order Taylor expansion of 1/(d ± R)².
  4. Moon (7.35×1022 kg, 3.84×108 m): atide = 1.1×10−6 m/s², 2.2× the Sun's. The Sun pulls ~180× harder overall, but tides go as M/d³, and the Moon is 390× closer.
  5. New and full moon: Sun, Earth and Moon line up, so the two tidal stretches add (1.6×10−6 vs. 0.6×10−6 m/s² at quarter moons, a factor ~2.7): spring tides. Full moon works as well as new moon because the stretch is symmetric.
  6. Jupiter at 4.2 AU (1.90×1027 kg): atide = 6.5×10−12 m/s², 6×10−6 of the Moon's and 1×10−5 of the Sun's. Scaling the ~0.5 m lunar tide gives a few micrometres, far below waves, weather and pressure changes: not measurable in the ocean.
Going deeper · energy bookkeeping in orbits (Owocki §7)
Virial theorem. For a circular orbit, kinetic energy K = GMm/2r, potential U = −GMm/r, so the total is E = U/2 = −K. Lose energy (drag) and the orbit shrinks and speeds up. The same bookkeeping says a contracting gas cloud radiates half of the released energy and heats up with the other half (Ch. 11, 13). Surface gravity spans huge ranges, so astronomers quote log g (cgs): red giant ≈ 0, Sun 4.4, Earth 3.0, white dwarf 8, neutron star 13. The difference from Earth's 3 is how many powers of ten heavier you'd be. Speeds: vesc = √(2GM/R): Earth 11.2, Moon 2.4, Sun 618 km/s; a low orbit needs vesc/√2 (7.9 km/s for Earth).
OwockiEscape speed from a star with the Sun's mass but 100× its radius? With 1/100 of its radius?
v ∝ √(M/R): 618/10 = 62 km/s (red giant, loses mass easily); 618×10 = 6200 km/s (white dwarf).
CosmosIf Earth shrank to half its radius with the same mass, your weight would…?
g ∝ M/R², so 4× larger. (Orbits around Earth would not change at all.)
OwockiOrbital speed at 100 AU from a Sun-like star? At 0.01 AU?

30-second
recap
Motion changes only when a net force acts (Newton 1–3). Angular momentum and energy are conserved, which keeps planets orbiting and makes collapsing clouds spin up and heat up. Gravity is F = GMm/d². Newton's form of Kepler 3 turns any orbit into a mass measurement; changing an orbit needs an energy exchange, and enough energy means escape. Tides are differences in gravity: two bulges, spring and neap tides, slowing Earth's spin and pushing the Moon away.
