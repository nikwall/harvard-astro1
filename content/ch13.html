13.1 Star birth

Stars form in molecular clouds: the coldest (10–30 K), densest parts of interstellar gas, where hydrogen forms H₂ molecules and dust blocks visible light (we see inside them with infrared and radio). Gravity must beat thermal pressure, and that's easiest when the gas is cold and dense and the cloud is massive. A collapsing cloud fragments into many clumps, so stars are born in clusters.

Each fragment heats up (gravitational energy → thermal energy), spins up and flattens (angular momentum): a protostar with a disk (where planets form, Ch. 6) and often two jets shooting out along the rotation axis. The protostar keeps contracting until its core reaches ~10 million K and hydrogen fusion begins: a main-sequence star. This takes ~50 Myr for the Sun, much less for massive stars.

Mass limits: above ~150 M☉, radiation pressure blows the star apart. Below 0.08 M☉, degeneracy pressure halts the collapse before the core gets hot enough for hydrogen fusion: a brown dwarf (a “failed star”, 13–80 Jupiter masses). Newborn stars are mostly low-mass: for every O star there are thousands of M stars.

Key insight · degeneracy pressure
Ordinary (thermal) pressure depends on temperature. Degeneracy pressure is quantum mechanical: no two electrons (or neutrons) can occupy the same state, so squeezing them into a smaller space forces them to move faster. It doesn't depend on temperature, so it can't act as a thermostat. It holds up brown dwarfs, the cores of red giants and white dwarfs (electron degeneracy), and neutron stars (neutron degeneracy).

13.2 Life as a low-mass star (below ~8 solar masses)

  1. Main sequence (~10 Gyr for the Sun): H → He in the core via the proton–proton chain, held steady by the thermostat.
  2. Core H runs out → red giant. The inert He core shrinks and heats (no fusion, so no support); hydrogen shell fusion ignites around it at a furious rate, and the outer layers swell to ~100× the Sun's radius and 1000× its luminosity.
  3. Helium flash: at ~100 million K, helium fuses into carbon (the triple-alpha process: 3 ⁴He → ¹²C). Because the core is degenerate it ignites almost explosively. Afterwards the star shrinks and dims somewhat, with helium core fusion plus hydrogen shell fusion.
  4. Double-shell burning (He and H shells around an inert carbon core): the star becomes a giant again, even bigger and more luminous (the AGB). Unstable shells drive strong winds.
  5. Death: the core never reaches carbon-fusion temperature, since degeneracy pressure stops the collapse. The outer layers drift off as a glowing planetary nebula (nothing to do with planets), exposing the hot core: a white dwarf (a carbon–oxygen ball the size of Earth), which cools forever.

The Sun's future: in ~5 Gyr it becomes a red giant, probably swallowing Mercury and Venus. Earth's fate is uncertain, but it will be scorched long before: the Sun brightens slowly, so oceans evaporate in ~1–2 Gyr.

13.3 Life as a high-mass star (above ~8 solar masses)

High-mass stars fuse hydrogen via the CNO cycle, with carbon, nitrogen and oxygen acting as catalysts. It's far faster, hence short lives. Afterward the core keeps contracting and heating, and each time a new fuel ignites: He → C, C → O, Ne, Mg, … up to Si → Fe. The star becomes a supergiant with an onion of fusion shells. Many elements form by adding helium nuclei, which is why elements with even numbers of protons (C, O, Ne, Mg, Si, S…) are more common: evidence that stars made them.

Iron is the dead end: it has the lowest mass per nuclear particle, so neither fusing nor splitting it releases energy. Iron piles up in an inert core supported only by electron degeneracy. When it passes ~1.4 M☉, gravity wins: in a fraction of a second electrons and protons merge into neutrons + neutrinos, the core collapses to a ball of neutrons ~20 km across and rebounds, and the outer layers blast away in a supernova (core collapse, “Type II”), briefly outshining a whole galaxy. It leaves a neutron star or, for the heaviest stars, a black hole. The expanding debris (a supernova remnant, like the Crab Nebula from 1054) seeds space with new elements. SN 1987A in the Large Magellanic Cloud confirmed the theory: detectors caught its neutrino burst hours before the light arrived.

13.4 Close binaries

When one star in a close pair swells into a giant, gas can spill onto its companion (mass transfer), rewriting both stars' futures. The Algol paradox: Algol's 3.7 M☉ star is still on the main sequence while its 0.8 M☉ partner is already a subgiant, even though massive stars evolve faster. Solution: the subgiant was originally the more massive star; it evolved first and gave away most of its mass. Mass transfer onto white dwarfs leads to novae and white-dwarf supernovae (Ch. 14).

Toolkit · conditions for star birth
$$M_{\min} = 18\,M_\odot\sqrt{\frac{T^3}{n}}$$T in K, n in particles/cm³: the minimum cloud mass for gravity to win over pressure (a form of the Jeans mass).
Worked: T = 30 K, n = 300 cm−3: Mmin = 18 × √(27,000/300) = 18 × 9.5 ≈ 171 M☉. A colder, denser core (10 K, 105 cm−3): 18 × √(10−2) = 1.8 M☉, the scale of single stars.

Practice Exam-style questions with worked answers

Quick QuizStars form most easily in clouds that are…? What is a brown dwarf?
Cold and dense. A brown dwarf is an object not quite massive enough to be a star (< 0.08 M☉).
Quick QuizHottest core: blue MS star, red supergiant, red MS star? No core fusion?
Hottest: the red supergiant (fusing heavy elements). No core fusion: the red giant (inert He core, H burns in a shell).
Quick QuizAfter the helium flash, luminosity…? And the core of a high-mass star after its H runs out?
Luminosity goes down (the star settles and shrinks). The core shrinks and heats up until the next fuel ignites.
Quick QuizIf H had the smallest mass per nuclear particle? If carbon did?
H lowest: no fusion in any star (no energy to gain). Carbon lowest: fusion would stop at C, iron cores could never build up, so no supernovae.
Quick QuizThe solar system in 10 Gyr contains…? Which 1 M☉ star dies first?
A white dwarf. Shortest life: the one with a 2 M☉ companion, which will dump mass onto it.
ProblemThe red-giant Sun reaches 1 AU in radius. Average density?
V = 4/3 π (1.5×1013 cm)³ ≈ 1.4×1040 cm³, so ρ = 2×1033 g / V ≈ 1.4×10−7 g/cm³: 107× thinner than water and 104× thinner than air.
ProblemBetelgeuse (643 ly) goes supernova at 1010 L☉. How much brighter than Sirius (26 L☉, 8.6 ly)?
b ∝ L/d²: (1010/26) × (8.6/643)² ≈ 7×104× brighter. It would be visible in daylight.
ProblemWhich elements come from C + C, C + Ne, Fe + He?
Add protons: 6+6 = 12 → Mg; 6+10 = 16 → S; 26+2 = 28 → Ni.
ProblemAlgol: 3.7 + 0.8 M☉, period 2.87 d. Separation?
a³ = (M1+M2) p² = 4.5 × (0.00786 yr)², so a ≈ 0.065 AU (≈14 R☉), much smaller than a red giant. So mass transfer is unavoidable.

Going further Activities, deeper physics & more practice

Hirshfeld Activities 15–16 · stellar lives from computer models (plot the tracks yourself)
The Sun (Act. 15, Standard Solar Model): it starts on the main sequence at 0.70 L☉ and brightens slowly to today's 1.00 L☉ at 4.6 Gyr. Core hydrogen runs out at ~11 Gyr. Then, in only ~0.7 Gyr (~7% of its life), it becomes a subgiant, a red giant (tip: ~2350 L☉, 3100 K, radius ~0.8 AU; then the helium flash), a horizontal-branch star, an AGB star (~5000 L☉), a planetary nebula, and a white dwarf of ~0.54 M☉. It sheds almost half its mass; with a weaker Sun, planetary orbits widen. Since most of a star's life is spent on the main sequence, most stars we see are there.
Massive stars (Act. 16, 15–40 M☉ models): they still spend ~85–90% of their lives on the main sequence, but the lives are short: ~7 Myr on the MS for 25 M☉, ~5 Myr for 40 M☉ (vs. ~1010 yr for the Sun), far too short for intelligent life on their planets. They swell to ~1000 R☉ ≈ 5 AU: placed at the Sun's position, Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars would all be inside. They lose a large fraction of their mass in winds; above ~40 M☉ the envelope is blown off, exposing a hot Wolf–Rayet star, which explodes from blue rather than red.
Going deeper · where the mass–luminosity law and the stellar mass limits come from (Owocki §17–19)
L ∝ M³ from two equations. Hydrostatic balance gives Tcore ∝ M/R; radiative diffusion gives L ∝ R T⁴/(κρ) with ρ ∝ M/R³. Combine: L ∝ M³, and the radius cancels. Eddington derived this in the 1920s, before anyone knew stars ran on fusion: luminosity is set by how fast energy can leak out, not by the nuclear furnace. Upper limit: light pushes on electrons; the Eddington luminosity LEdd = 4πGMc/κ ≈ 3.8×104 L☉ (M/M☉). Since L grows as M³, stars above ~150–200 M☉ would blow themselves apart. Lower limit: below 0.08 M☉ electron degeneracy stops the contraction before the core reaches ~10 MK: a brown dwarf (it briefly fuses deuterium). Pre-main sequence: a fully convective protostar slides down at ~3500–4000 K (the Hayashi track), then moves left at nearly constant L to the main sequence.
OwockiEddington luminosity of a 60 M☉ star? Its expected L ∝ M³?
LEdd = 3.8×104 × 60 ≈ 2×106 L☉; 60³ ≈ 2×105 L☉: close to the limit already. More massive stars shed mass violently.
OwockiA cluster's main-sequence turnoff is at 100 L☉. Its age (t ≈ 10 Gyr (L/L☉)−2/3)?
10 Gyr × 100−2/3 = 10/21.5 ≈ 0.5 Gyr.
CosmosSN 1987A: ~20 neutrinos were caught in Japan and the US hours before the light. Why does that matter?
It confirmed core collapse: 99% of the energy leaves as neutrinos from the new neutron star. They escape at once; light waits for the shock to reach the surface.

30-second
recap
Mass is destiny. Stars form from cold, dense molecular clouds (0.08–150 M☉; below that, brown dwarfs). Low-mass stars: main sequence → red giant (H shell) → helium flash and He fusion → double-shell giant → planetary nebula + white dwarf. High-mass stars: CNO cycle → successive fusion up to iron (the dead end) → core collapse → supernova → neutron star or black hole, spreading the elements of life. Degeneracy pressure sets the endpoints; close binaries swap mass.
