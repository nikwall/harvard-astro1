6.2–6.3 The nebular theory explains all four

The solar system formed from the gravitational collapse of an interstellar cloud (the solar nebula), itself the product of billions of years of galactic recycling. The nebula was 98% hydrogen and helium, 1.4% hydrogen compounds (water, methane, ammonia), 0.4% rock and 0.2% metal. As it collapsed, three things happened:

Heatinggravitational potential energy → thermal energy (hottest near the center → the Sun)
Spinningconservation of angular momentum: a smaller cloud must spin faster
Flatteningcollisions between clumps average out random motions → a spinning disk

Pattern 1 explained: the planets were born in that spinning disk, so they inherit its direction and plane. Disks like this are seen today around young stars (ALMA's image of HL Tau shows rings and gaps carved by forming planets).

Pattern 2 explained by temperature, not gravity: as the disk cooled, materials condensed into solid grains wherever it was cold enough. Inside the frost line (between today's Mars and Jupiter orbits), only metal and rock condensed, so the terrestrial planets grew from a scarce supply by accretion: grains → pebbles → kilometer-size planetesimals → planets. They stayed small and couldn't hold hydrogen gas. Beyond the frost line, ice was abundant, so planetesimals grew big and fast. Once massive enough (~10 Earth masses), their gravity captured hydrogen and helium gas directly from the nebula, making the jovian planets, each with a mini-disk that formed its moons.

Clearing the nebula: the young Sun's strong solar wind blew away the leftover gas, ending gas capture and fixing the planets' compositions.

Pattern 3 explained: asteroids are leftover rocky planetesimals of the inner system; comets are leftover icy planetesimals, flung outward by the jovian planets into the Kuiper belt and Oort cloud. Most craters date from the heavy bombardment of the first few hundred million years, which may also have delivered Earth's water.

Pattern 4 explained: random giant impacts and close encounters. The giant impact hypothesis for our Moon: a Mars-size planetesimal hit the young Earth, and debris from the outer (rocky, iron-poor) layers formed the Moon. That explains the Moon's low iron content and its composition matching Earth's mantle, and computer models reproduce it. Captured moons explain odd orbits like Triton's.

6.4 How old is the solar system?

Radiometric dating measures when a rock last solidified. A radioactive parent isotope decays into a daughter at a fixed rate (the half-life, e.g., potassium-40 → argon-40 in 1.25 Gyr; uranium-238 → lead in 4.5 Gyr). Gaseous argon escapes a molten rock, so all the argon trapped in a solid rock came from decay after it solidified. The oldest Earth rocks are ~4 Gyr old, Moon rocks up to 4.4 Gyr, and the oldest meteorites 4.56 Gyr. That marks the start of accretion: the solar system's age.

Toolkit · radiometric age
$$t = t_{\text{half}} \times \frac{\log_{10}\!\left(\dfrac{\text{current amount}}{\text{original amount}}\right)}{\log_{10}\!\left(\tfrac12\right)}$$
Worked: a meteorite holds 0.850 units of K-40 and 9.15 units of Ar-40, so it started with 10.0 units of K-40. t = 1.25 Gyr × log(0.085)/log(0.5) = 1.25 × 3.56 = 4.45 Gyr.
Course link · L8 “Formation of stars and their planetary systems”
The lecture treats star and planet formation as one process. A cloud core collapses (Ch. 13 covers the star's side); angular momentum forces most of the material into a protoplanetary disk; the protostar grows at the center while the disk makes planets within a few million years, before the gas is gone. Watch for these modern twists: (1) pebble accretion and streaming instabilities speed up planetesimal growth; (2) migration: young planets exchange angular momentum with the disk and drift inward or outward, which explains “hot Jupiters” (Ch. 10) and suggests Jupiter and Saturn rearranged the early solar system; (3) disks are now imaged directly (ALMA's HL Tau, 2014), showing gaps where planets clear lanes.
Myth vs. reality
✗ The Sun's gravity pulled the dense rock inward. ✓ Everything orbited together; orbits don't depend on density. Temperature (condensation) sorted the planets.
✗ Order of steps: accretion, then condensation. ✓ Collapse → condensation → accretion: grains must exist before they can stick together.

Practice Exam-style questions with worked answers

Quick QuizHow many planets orbit the Sun in Earth's direction? What shape are their orbits?
All of them, on fairly circular orbits in nearly the same plane: the fingerprint of a spinning disk.
Quick QuizWhich did NOT happen as the nebula collapsed: spin-up, heating, or concentrating dense material near the Sun?
Concentrating dense material didn't happen. Heating and spin-up did (energy and angular momentum conservation).
Quick QuizJupiter's main ingredient? The solar nebula's?
Hydrogen and helium for both (the nebula was 98% H/He). Jupiter captured nebular gas.
Quick QuizWhat's unusual about our Moon? Which planets are spin exceptions?
It's surprisingly large relative to its planet (giant impact). Exceptions: Venus (backward spin) and Uranus (98° tilt).
Visual SkillsU-235 (half-life 0.7 Gyr): fraction left after 3.5 Gyr? A rock with 60% converted to lead is how old?
3.5/0.7 = 5 half-lives, so 1/32 is left. 40% left: t = 0.7 × log 0.4/log 0.5 ≈ 0.93 Gyr, a volcanic rock nearly a billion years old.
ProblemK-40 dating: equal K-40 and Ar-40? Three times as much Ar as K?
Half left → one half-life = 1.25 Gyr. ¼ left → two half-lives = 2.5 Gyr.
ProblemMoon rock with 55% of its U-238 remaining (half-life 4.5 Gyr)?
t = 4.5 × log(0.55)/log(0.5) = 4.5 × 0.86 ≈ 3.9 Gyr.
ProblemC-14 (5700 yr): dye with 77% left? Wood with 6.2%? Can C-14 date Earth?
0.38 half-lives ≈ 2100 yr ago; 4.0 half-lives ≈ 2.3×104 yr ago. No for Earth: after ~10 half-lives (~60,000 yr) none is left.
ProblemIf planets could orbit in either direction at random, the odds all 8 go the same way (all heads)?
(½)8 = 1/256 (≈ 0.4%). The observed order strongly favors formation in one spinning disk.

Going further Deeper physics, other views & more practice

Going deeper · from cloud to disk to planets, with numbers (Owocki §22–23)
  1. Collapse or not? A cloud collapses if gravity beats thermal pressure (the Jeans criterion, virial theorem). The minimum mass grows like T3/2/√n: for cold atomic gas (100 K, 10 atoms/cm³) it is ~3×104 M☉. That's why stars form in clusters, from giant molecular clouds that later fragment (the guide's 18 M☉√(T³/n) is the same law, for molecular gas).
  2. How fast? Free-fall time tff ≈ 51 Myr/√n (n in cm−3): ~5 Myr for n = 100. But the Galaxy turns only ~1% of that gas into stars per free-fall time (magnetic fields, turbulence and feedback from young stars slow it down).
  3. Why a disk? Material near the rotation axis falls straight in; material near the equator has too much angular momentum and settles into an orbiting disk. Observed cores (~0.05 pc, slowly spinning) give disks of a few hundred AU, just what ALMA sees: HL Tau's disk is ~200 AU across, with gaps cut by forming planets, in a star <1 Myr old.
  4. A race against time: gas disks disappear in a few Myr (accretion onto the star, evaporation by starlight), so giant planets must grab their gas quickly.
  5. The ice line: a body at distance d from the Sun reaches T ≈ 280 K/√(d/AU), whatever its size (the radius cancels). Water ice survives below ~150 K, i.e. beyond ~3–5 AU in the young, cooler Sun. Beyond it, solids were ~3× more plentiful, so cores grew big enough (~10 M⊕) to capture hydrogen and helium.
From the other books · planet-formation facts worth knowing
Two routes to giant planets (Pasachoff & Filippenko ch. 9): core accretion (a rocky-icy core first, then a gas envelope: the favoured model) vs. disk instability (a massive disk fragments directly into giant planets: may explain giants far from their stars). Angular momentum: the Sun has 99.9% of the mass, but the planets carry ~98% of the angular momentum (Jupiter alone ~60%). Outward angular-momentum transport in the disk (friction between faster inner and slower outer rings) let most mass fall into the Sun. Debris disks like β Pictoris's are the dusty leftovers seen around stars ~10–100 Myr old.

CosmosWhy does Jupiter's composition resemble the Sun's, while Earth's doesn't?

recap
A collapsing cloud heats, spins up and flattens into a disk, so the planets orbit in one direction and one plane. Temperature sorted the ingredients: inside the frost line only metal and rock condensed (small terrestrial planets); beyond it ices condensed too, making big cores that captured H/He (jovian planets). Leftover planetesimals are asteroids (rocky) and comets (icy). Giant impacts explain the exceptions, including our Moon. Radiometric dating of meteorites gives 4.56 Gyr.
