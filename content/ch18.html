18.1–18.2 Unseen influences and the evidence for dark matter

Dark matter: unseen mass inferred only from its gravity. Dark energy: whatever makes the expansion accelerate. Neither has been directly detected; each is the simplest explanation for a set of observed motions.

1. Galaxies: Vera Rubin (1970s) measured spiral-galaxy rotation curves with Doppler shifts, and 21-cm radio extends them past the visible disk. The curves stay flat, so by M = rv²/G the enclosed mass keeps rising even where there's almost no light. Typical spirals have ≥10× more dark matter than stars, spread through a huge halo.

2. Clusters of galaxies, with three independent methods that agree: (a) galaxy orbital speeds (Fritz Zwicky, 1933: “missing mass”); (b) the temperature of the hot X-ray gas (107–108 K), which stays bound only if the cluster is very massive; (c) gravitational lensing: the cluster's mass bends light from background galaxies into arcs (Einstein). All give dark matter ≥40× the stellar mass. In the Bullet Cluster, a collision separated the hot gas from the lensing mass, which is hard to explain without real dark matter.

Is it real? The alternative is that our law of gravity is wrong on large scales (e.g., “MOND”). That can't be excluded, but general relativity passes every precise test, and a single dark-matter model explains galaxies, clusters, lensing and the CMB together.

What is it? Some could be ordinary baryonic matter that's dim (brown dwarfs, rogue planets, stellar remnants), but Big Bang deuterium and the CMB show that ordinary matter is only ~1/6 of all matter. The rest must be nonbaryonic: most likely undiscovered WIMPs (weakly interacting massive particles) that feel gravity but not light. Axions are another candidate. Neutrinos are too light and fast. Detectors deep underground (e.g., LZ) haven't caught them yet.

18.3 Structure formation

Dark matter doesn't interact with light, so after the Big Bang it could start clumping before ordinary matter, which radiation kept smooth until the CMB was released. The slightly denser regions seeded by inflation grew under dark matter's gravity; ordinary gas then fell into these dark matter halos, cooled and formed galaxies. On the largest scales galaxies trace a cosmic web of filaments and sheets around vast voids hundreds of millions of ly across. Supercomputer simulations (N-body codes that follow billions of particles, the topic of the Cosmological computing section) that start from the CMB ripples reproduce this web statistically. That's one of the strongest arguments that the whole picture hangs together.

18.4 Dark energy and the fate of the universe

The surprise of 1998: two teams used white-dwarf supernovae to measure how expansion changed over time. Distant supernovae looked fainter than expected, so they are farther away, so the expansion has been speeding up for the last ~5 billion years (Nobel Prize 2011: Perlmutter, Schmidt, Riess).

A second, independent argument: flat geometry. The CMB shows space is flat, which (in general relativity) requires the total density to equal the critical density. All matter adds up to only ~30% of it, so ~70% must be something else: dark energy, the same amount the supernovae require.

What is it? Unknown. The simplest option is Einstein's cosmological constant, an energy of empty space itself (his “greatest blunder”, which he had added to keep the universe static). Fate: if dark energy stays constant, expansion accelerates forever. Galaxies beyond our Local Group will eventually recede beyond view, stars will burn out, and the universe grows cold and dark (a “big freeze”). Recent DESI survey results (2024–25) hint that dark energy may be slowly weakening, which is still under debate.

ProblemA spiral rotates at 200 km/s at both 30,000 and 60,000 ly. Enclosed masses?
M = rv²/G: 2.8×1020 m × 4×1010/G ≈ 1.7×1041 kg ≈ 8.5×1010 M☉, then double at 60,000 ly. Mass grows ∝ r where there's little light: dark halo.
ProblemA cluster: r = 4.8×1022 m, gas at 6×107 K, v ≈ 140 m/s × √T, L = 8×1012 L☉. Mass and M/L?
v ≈ 140 × 7.7×10³ ≈ 1.1×106 m/s, so M = rv²/G ≈ 8×1044 kg ≈ 4×1014 M☉, and M/L ≈ 50.
Visual SkillsOn a graph of galaxy separation vs. time, how do you read acceleration?
The slope is the expansion rate. Slope increasing (curving up) = accelerating; straight = coasting; flattening = decelerating.

Going further Deeper physics, history & more practice

Going deeper · how we know the expansion accelerates (Owocki §29–31)
  1. Standard candles. White-dwarf (Type Ia) supernovae all explode near 1.4 M☉, so their peak luminosity is nearly the same (~1010 L☉, M ≈ −19.3). From m, d = 10(m − M + 5)/5 pc; a survey reaching m = 24 finds them ~5 Gpc away.
  2. The 1998 surprise. Distant supernovae were fainter (farther) than even an empty, coasting universe predicts. So the expansion is speeding up, driven by something with repulsive gravity: dark energy (behaving like Einstein's cosmological constant Λ, which he had once called his “greatest blunder”). Nobel Prize 2011 (Perlmutter, Schmidt, Riess; Filippenko was on both teams).
  3. When did acceleration start? Matter's pull dilutes as 1/R³; Λ doesn't dilute. In a flat universe with Ωm = 0.3, ΩΛ = 0.7, deceleration flips to acceleration at R = (Ωm/2ΩΛ)1/3 ≈ 0.6, i.e. z ≈ 0.7, about 6 billion years ago. The two effects nearly cancel over time, so the age is 0.96/H0 ≈ 13.8 Gyr.
  4. What dark matter isn't. MACHOs (dim compact halo objects) would occasionally microlens background stars; surveys found far too few events. The favoured candidates are non-atomic particles (e.g. WIMPs), not yet detected directly.
From The Cosmos · the far future of an accelerating universe
If the expansion continues forever: the stelliferous era (now) lasts until the last red dwarfs die, ~1014 yr; then a degenerate era of white dwarfs, neutron stars and brown dwarfs; then a black-hole era once ordinary matter decays (if protons are unstable); finally a dark era, after even black holes have evaporated by Hawking radiation, leaving a cold, nearly empty space of low-energy photons and particles. Meanwhile, acceleration carries other galaxy groups beyond our view: future astronomers in the merged Milky Way–Andromeda galaxy would see only their own galaxy.

CosmosΩM = 0.3: if dark energy did not exist, what would the geometry be, and the fate?
Ω < 1: open (saddle-shaped) and expanding forever, ever more slowly. With ΩΛ = 0.7 the total is 1: flat, as the CMB shows.
CosmosTrue or false: E = mc² means dark matter and dark energy are the same thing.
False. Dark matter clumps and pulls (attractive gravity); dark energy is smooth and makes the expansion accelerate (repulsive).

30-second
recap
Dark matter (~27%) shows up only through gravity: flat rotation curves, cluster orbits, hot gas and lensing. Most of it isn't made of atoms (deuterium and the CMB say ordinary matter is ~1/6 of all matter), so it's likely undiscovered particles. Its gravity built the cosmic web of filaments and voids. Dark energy (~68%) is revealed by supernovae (accelerating expansion) and by flat geometry (matter is only 30% of critical). Likely fate: expansion forever, a cold dark future. Atoms: 5%.
