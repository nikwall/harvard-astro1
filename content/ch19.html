19.1 Life on Earth

Life started early: stromatolite fossils show microbes ≥3.5 billion years ago, and carbon isotopes suggest >3.85 Gyr, within a few hundred million years of Earth's formation and right after the heavy bombardment. That hints that life arises readily when conditions allow.

How? Genetic comparison of all organisms gives one tree of life with three domains (bacteria, archaea, eukarya); plants and animals are two small branches. The root points to a common ancestor resembling today's heat-loving microbes at deep-sea hydrothermal vents. Lab work (the 1953 Miller–Urey experiment, RNA chemistry) shows that the building blocks form naturally, and meteorites carry amino acids. The exact origin is unknown. Once started, life diversified by evolution through natural selection, a well-tested scientific theory. Photosynthesis slowly filled the air with oxygen (~2.4 Gyr ago) and built the ozone layer. Before that, UV made land surfaces lethal, so early life stayed in water.

Necessities of life (as we know it): a source of nutrients, a source of energy (sunlight or chemical), and liquid water. Extremophiles thrive in boiling vents, acid, ice and deep rock, which greatly widens where we might look.

19.2 Life in the solar system?

  • Mars: it had liquid water early (Ch. 7), so life may have started and might survive in subsurface water. Perseverance is collecting samples in an ancient lake delta; the rock “Cheyava Falls” (sampled 2024, analysis published 2025) shows chemical patterns that could be biosignatures, but nothing is confirmed.
  • Europa (ocean under ice, in contact with rock: Europa Clipper arrives 2030), Enceladus (geysers containing water, organics and phosphorus), Ganymede and Callisto (possible deep oceans), Titan (liquid methane lakes: life with a different solvent? Dragonfly is headed there), even Triton and Pluto.
  • Not the Moon or Mercury: no liquid, no atmosphere, no geology.

19.3 Life around other stars

Surface habitability needs a planet in its star's habitable zone, plus Earth's other gifts: enough size for volcanism (atmosphere), plate tectonics (the CO₂ thermostat, Ch. 7) and a magnetic field (shielding the air). Around M dwarfs (most stars) the zone is close in, so planets may be tidally locked and blasted by flares. Longer-lived, stable stars are best. Subsurface habitats (like Europa's) could be even more common, and don't need a habitable zone at all.

How common? Kepler's statistics suggest that roughly 1 in 5 Sun-like stars (estimates range widely) has an Earth-size planet in its habitable zone, so ~1010 such planets in the Milky Way.

Detecting life: even a few pixels could show oceans and seasons, but spectra are the key. We look for biosignatures: gases out of chemical balance, such as oxygen/ozone together with methane, which life keeps replenishing. JWST can probe atmospheres of some small planets; claims of dimethyl sulfide on K2-18b (2023–25) remain contested. NASA's planned Habitable Worlds Observatory aims to image Earth-like exoplanets directly.

The Drake equation (1961) doesn't give an answer; it organizes our ignorance. Only NHP is now roughly known. Everything hinges on the biology and sociology terms.

SETI searches for artificial radio or laser signals (Breakthrough Listen and others). Nothing confirmed so far, though only a tiny fraction of stars and frequencies have been searched.

Interstellar travel is hard: at 10% of c, a trip to Alpha Centauri takes ~44 years, and the energy needed is enormous (below). Radiation, engines and shielding are serious problems, but none clearly violates physics (light sails, fusion, antimatter).

Fermi's paradox: a civilization with even slow starships could colonize the galaxy in a few million years, and the galaxy had billions of years before us. So where is everybody? Possible answers, each with profound implications: (1) we are alone or nearly so (life or intelligence is rare, a “Rare Earth”); (2) civilizations are common but don't expand: they lose interest or destroy themselves; (3) there is a galactic civilization that deliberately stays hidden (the “zoo” hypothesis).

Course link · L23 “The cosmic inventory”: the whole course in one breath
The Big Bang made hydrogen and helium (Ch. 17); dark matter gathered them into galaxies (Ch. 18, 16). Stars fused them into carbon, oxygen and iron and returned them to space through winds, planetary nebulae and supernovae; neutron-star mergers added gold (Ch. 13–14). Galactic recycling enriched the gas (Ch. 15) until a cloud collapsed into the Sun and a disk, where rock condensed inside the frost line (Ch. 6–7). On one mid-sized planet, at the right distance, with water, plate tectonics and a magnetic field, chemistry became biology, and 4 billion years later biology built telescopes. We learned all of this with gravity and light (Ch. 4–5), and are only ~5% of the cosmic budget.

Practice Exam-style questions with worked answers

Quick QuizWhen did life arise on Earth? What are plants and animals?
Within a few hundred million years after Earth formed. Plants and animals are two small branches of a microbe-dominated tree of life.
Quick QuizWhy couldn't early life survive on land? Key requirement for life?
There was no ozone layer (deadly UV). The key requirement: liquid water (not photosynthesis or ozone).
Quick QuizNot a candidate for life: Europa, Mars, the Moon? A G star's habitable zone vs. an M star's?
The Moon. A G star's zone is larger (and farther out) because the star is more luminous.
Quick Quizflife = ½ means…? A civilization millions of years ahead of us is…?
Half of habitable worlds have life. Such a civilization is one reasonable answer to Fermi's paradox: neither certain nor impossible.
Quick QuizEnergy to accelerate a large ship to 0.5c?
More than 2000× the world's annual energy use (not a supernova, but far beyond today's means).
Problem10,000 broadcasting civilizations among 5×1011 stars. How many stars to search per signal? With 100?
5×1011/104 = 5×107 stars; with 100: 5×109. A patient search.
ProblemArecibo (300 m) hears a 10 kW signal from 100 ly. What dish size is needed at 70,000 ly?
Flux ∝ 1/d², so the needed area ∝ d² and the diameter ∝ d: 300 m × 700 = 210 km.
ProblemA cruise ship (108 kg) at 0.1c: kinetic energy? Cost at 5¢/MJ?
½ × 108 × (3×107)² = 4.5×1022 J, about the world's annual use (5×1022 J). Cost 4.5×1016 MJ × $0.05 ≈ $2×1015.
Problem…using matter–antimatter annihilation instead: how much antimatter?
mtotal = E/c² = 4.5×1022/9×1016 = 5×105 kg, half of it antimatter: 2.5×105 kg. We make nanograms per year.

Going further Activities, deeper physics & more practice

Hirshfeld Activity 25 · swap the Sun for another star
The flux at Earth is S = L/(4πd²), and the no-greenhouse temperature is T = [S(1 − a)/(4σ)]1/4 with a = 0.30 (Ch. 7).
  1. An A0 star (80 L☉) at 1 AU: S = 80 × 1370 = 1.1×105 W/m², T ≈ 760 K (490 °C). Oceans boil away: uninhabitable (and an A star lives only ~1 Gyr).
  2. An M0 star (0.06 L☉) at 1 AU: S = 82 W/m², T ≈ 126 K (−147 °C): a frozen world.
  3. Where would Earth have to be? Keeping S fixed needs d ∝ √L: √80 ≈ 8.9 AU for the A0 star (near Saturn's orbit) and √0.06 ≈ 0.24 AU for the M0 star (inside Mercury's orbit). The habitable zone moves outward for bright stars, inward for dim ones.
Temperature isn't everything: size, atmosphere, greenhouse strength (Venus!) and the star's steadiness matter too.
Going deeper · habitable planets around red dwarfs, and life under ice (Owocki §24–25)
For a star with half the Sun's mass and temperature, an Earth-temperature orbit has a period of only ~65 days at ~0.25 AU. Such planets are easier to detect (bigger wobble, deeper transit, frequent repeats), which is why many habitable-zone candidates orbit red dwarfs (TRAPPIST-1, Proxima b). The catches: they are likely tidally locked (one side always facing the star), and young red dwarfs flare violently, which can strip atmospheres. Beyond the habitable zone, tidal flexing heats Europa and Enceladus enough for oceans under their ice; Cassini flew through Enceladus's geysers and sampled salty water with organic molecules. On Earth, deep-sea vent ecosystems live on chemical energy rather than sunlight. A transit spectrum showing O2 together with methane would be a strong biosignature, since each destroys the other unless life keeps replenishing them.
CosmosA ship travels at 0.97c; the trip takes 30 years by Earth's clocks. How long for the crew?
γ = 1/√(1 − 0.97²) = 4.1, so 30/4.1 ≈ 7.3 years (time dilation). The energy cost is enormous (see the problems above).
CosmosAt 100 km/s (a fast spacecraft), how long to a star 20 ly away?
1.9×1014 km / 100 km/s = 1.9×1012 s ≈ 60,000 years.
Cosmos10% of stars are suitable, 30% of those have planets, 20% of systems have one at the right distance. How many in a 1011-star galaxy?
0.1 × 0.3 × 0.2 = 0.6% → ~6×108 candidate stars. The Drake-equation terms that follow are the truly uncertain ones.

30-second
recap
Life on Earth appeared early (>3.5 Gyr ago), shares one common ancestor, and needs only nutrients, energy and liquid water. Candidates nearby: Mars (past), Europa and Enceladus (oceans), Titan. Beyond: habitable-zone planets are common, and spectra could reveal biosignatures such as O₂ + CH₄. The Drake equation frames the unknowns; SETI hasn't heard anyone; interstellar travel is hard but not impossible; and Fermi's paradox asks why no one is here. Any answer changes how we see ourselves.
