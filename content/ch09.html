9.1 Classifying small bodies

Definitions
Asteroid · comet
Leftover planetesimals: rocky (asteroid) or ice-rich (comet).
Dwarf planet
Big enough to be round, but hasn't cleared its orbit: Ceres (asteroid belt), Pluto and Eris (Kuiper belt).
Meteor · meteorite
A meteor is the flash of a sand-grain-to-pebble-size particle burning up in the air (a “shooting star”). A meteorite is a rock that survives and lands.
Meteor shower
Earth crossing a comet's debris trail (e.g., the Perseids in August from Comet Swift–Tuttle).

9.2 Asteroids and meteorites

Asteroids range from Ceres (~940 km) down to boulders. Small ones are far more numerous and are usually lumpy, since their gravity is too weak to make them round. Spacecraft find many are rubble piles, and some have moons. Outer-belt asteroids are darker and richer in carbon and water. All of them together have less mass than our Moon, and the belt is mostly empty space: no dodging needed.

Why is there a belt and not a planet? Orbital resonances with Jupiter kept stirring up planetesimals there, so collisions shattered rather than merged them, and material was ejected. The region lost most of its mass. Resonances still clear gaps (Kirkwood gaps) and occasionally send fragments toward Earth.

Meteorites are free samples of asteroids. Primitive meteorites (chondrites) are unchanged since the solar system's birth: they contain the first solids (dated 4.567 Gyr) and even amino acids. Processed meteorites (irons, stony-irons, basaltic ones) come from asteroids that differentiated. An iron meteorite is a piece of a shattered asteroid's core. Some meteorites come from the Moon and Mars. Sample-return missions (Hayabusa2 from Ryugu, OSIRIS-REx from Bennu, 2023) found water-bearing minerals and organics.

9.3 Comets

A comet nucleus is a “dirty snowball” a few km across: ices mixed with dark dust and organics. Far from the Sun it's frozen and inactive. Coming inward, ices sublimate and gas and dust stream off into a huge coma and two tails: the plasma tail (ionized gas pushed straight out by the solar wind) and the dust tail (pushed by sunlight, curving along the orbit). Each passage erodes the comet; larger debris spreads along the orbit and makes meteor showers.

Two reservoirs: Kuiper belt comets formed where they are, beyond Neptune, in a flattened, orderly disk. Oort cloud comets (~1012) formed among the jovian planets and were flung out by gravitational encounters, so their orbits are random in tilt and very elongated. Passing stars nudge a few back inward as long-period comets.

9.4 Pluto and the Kuiper belt

Pluto (2370 km) is an ice-rich world, the best-known large Kuiper belt object, far more like a comet than a planet. Its orbit is in a 3:2 resonance with Neptune: sometimes it's closer to the Sun than Neptune, but they can never collide. Its big moon Charon (half Pluto's size) likely formed in a giant impact. New Horizons (2015) revealed a surprisingly active world: nitrogen-ice glaciers (Sputnik Planitia), water-ice mountains and a thin, hazy atmosphere. Eris (2005), slightly more massive than Pluto, triggered the 2006 IAU vote: a planet must have cleared its orbital neighborhood, which Pluto hasn't. Neptune's Triton is probably a captured Kuiper belt object.

9.5 Cosmic collisions: small bodies vs. the planets

Did an impact kill the dinosaurs? The mass extinction 65 million years ago coincides with a worldwide clay layer rich in iridium (rare on Earth's surface, common in asteroids), shocked quartz and soot, and with the ~180 km Chicxulub crater under Mexico's Yucatán coast, of exactly the right age. A ~10 km object would have raised dust and soot that darkened and cooled the planet. ~75% of species died, clearing the way for mammals.

Risk today: Tunguska (1908, a ~40 m object exploding over Siberia and flattening 2000 km² of forest) happens every few centuries, and Chelyabinsk (2013, ~20 m, 1500 injured by broken glass) every few decades. A 1 km impact would bring widespread devastation and climate change. Survey telescopes track near-Earth objects, and NASA's DART mission (2022) deliberately hit the asteroid Dimorphos and changed its orbit, the first test of planetary defense.

Jupiter's double role: its gravity shaped the asteroid belt, the Kuiper belt and the Oort cloud, and it still decides when objects are flung our way. It also ejects many comets that would otherwise hit us.

Toolkit · impacts are about kinetic energy
$$E_k = \tfrac12 m v^2,\qquad 1\ \text{megaton TNT} = 4.2\times10^{15}\ \text{J}$$Impact speeds are 10–70 km/s, so even small objects carry enormous energy. Volume (and mass) scale as D³.
Worked: a 2 km comet (4.2×1012 kg) at 30 km/s: E = ½ × 4.2×1012 × (3×104)² = 1.9×1021 J ≈ 4.5×105 Mt, the energy of ~30 million Hiroshima bombs (15 kt each), enough to blast a ~20 km crater.

Practice Exam-style questions with worked answers

Quick QuizHow does Jupiter nudge asteroids? Did a planet ever form in the belt?
Through orbital resonances. No planet formed: Jupiter prevented accretion by stirring the orbits.
Quick QuizCan an asteroid be pure metal?
Yes: it's a piece of the core of a shattered, differentiated asteroid. No region of the nebula condensed only metal.
Quick QuizWhat does Pluto most resemble? What makes a typical shooting star?
A comet (a large icy Kuiper belt object). A shooting star is a sand grain or small pebble.
Quick QuizMost elliptical and tilted orbits? Which formed farthest from the Sun?
Oort cloud comets have the most random orbits. Kuiper belt comets formed farthest out; Oort comets formed among the giants and were flung out.
Quick QuizHow often does a 1 km object hit Earth, and with what effect?
About once per million years, causing widespread devastation and climate change (not a mass extinction: that takes ~10 km).
ProblemCombine 106 asteroids of 1 km diameter. Size? How many to make Earth?
Volume ×106 → diameter × (106)1/3 = 100 km. Earth: (12,756)³ ≈ 2×1012 such asteroids.
ProblemToutatis passed 1.5×106 km from Earth. If it had passed at a random point within that radius, what are the odds of a hit?
Target area ratio (R⊕/1.5×106 km)² = (6378/1.5×106)² ≈ 1.8×10−5 (1 in 55,000). Hardly a “near miss”.
Problem1012 Oort comets within 50,000 AU. Typical spacing?
V = 4/3 π (5×104)³ ≈ 5.2×1014 AU³, or 520 AU³ each; the cube root gives ~8 AU apart: Sun-to-Saturn distances between comets.
ProblemEarth gains a few hundred tons (3×105 kg) of dust a day. Time to gain 0.1% of its mass?
6×1021 kg ÷ 3×105 kg/day = 2×1016 days ≈ 5×1013 yr, far longer than the universe's age. Insignificant.

Going further Activities, deeper physics & more practice

Hirshfeld Activity 24 (comet part) · how hot is Halley's nucleus?
Giotto (1986) found Halley's nucleus to be a potato-shaped, 16 × 8 km lump of ice and dust coated in dark organics: albedo a = 0.04 (it absorbs 96% of sunlight, darker than coal). At perihelion, d = 0.6 AU, so S = 1370/0.6² = 3800 W/m².
Worked: T = [S(1 − a)/(4σ)]1/4 = [3800 × 0.96 / (4 × 5.67×10−8)]1/4 ≈ 356 K ≈ 80 °C, close to what Giotto measured (roughly 300–400 K).
Its fate: far above freezing, ices sublimate straight to gas every 76-year pass, feeding the coma and tails. Each pass strips a surface layer, so Halley will eventually run out of ice: an inert, dark rock, or it breaks up into a meteoroid stream (Halley's debris already gives the Orionids in October and the η Aquariids in May). At 5 AU the same formula gives ~120 K: ices stay frozen, which is why comets grow tails only inside ~3 AU.
Going deeper · weighing Pluto with Charon (Kepler 3, Ch. 4)
Charon orbits Pluto every 6.387 days at 19,600 km. So M = 4π²a³/(GP²) = 39.5 × (1.96×107 m)³ / [6.67×10−11 × (5.52×105 s)²] = 1.5×1022 kg for Pluto + Charon: only 1/400 of Earth, far too little to disturb Uranus. The famous “prediction” of Pluto was luck. Pluto itself (1.3×1022 kg, radius 1190 km) has a density of ~1.9 g/cm³: a rock-ice mix like other Kuiper-belt objects. In 2005 Eris turned out as massive, prompting the 2006 “dwarf planet” category. Pluto emits mostly in the far infrared (40 K → Wien peak ~70 μm).
From The Cosmos · small bodies
Two tails: a curved dust tail (pushed by sunlight) and a straight, bluish ion tail (blown by the solar wind); both point away from the Sun, so a departing comet travels tail-first. Showers happen when Earth crosses an old comet's debris stream; the meteors seem to radiate from one point (the radiant) by perspective. Most meteorites found are iron (they look unusual and resist weathering), but most falls are stony. NEAR Shoemaker landed on Eros (2001); Deep Impact hit comet Tempel 1 (2005); Comet Shoemaker–Levy 9 hit Jupiter in 1994, leaving Earth-sized scars.

Mass ∝ size³, so 1000× the kinetic energy. Chicxulub (~10 km) released ~108 megatons.
