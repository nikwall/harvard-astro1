8.1 A different kind of planet

Composition: Jupiter and Saturn are almost entirely hydrogen and helium, like the Sun. Uranus and Neptune have proportionally more hydrogen compounds (water, methane, ammonia) plus rock and metal. None has a solid surface: gas simply gets denser and hotter with depth. Deep inside Jupiter and Saturn, pressure turns hydrogen into metallic hydrogen, which conducts electricity. With rapid rotation (Jupiter's day is ~10 h), that powers huge magnetospheres (Jupiter's would look bigger than the full Moon in our sky).

More mass ≠ much bigger. Add mass to a hydrogen planet and gravity compresses it; a planet several times Jupiter's mass would be about the same size. Jupiter, Saturn and Neptune emit more energy than they absorb, from slow contraction (turning gravitational energy into heat) and, in Saturn, helium “rain” (differentiation). It is not fusion: they're far too small.

Weather: layered clouds of ammonia, ammonium hydrosulfide and water give Jupiter its bands and colors. The Great Red Spot, a storm larger than Earth, has lasted at least 350 years. Neptune has the fastest winds (~2000 km/h). Uranus and Neptune look blue because methane absorbs red light. Uranus, tipped 98°, has 42-year-long polar days and nights.

8.2 A wealth of worlds: moons of ice and rock

Moons come small (<300 km, potato-shaped, often captured asteroids or comets), medium (300–1500 km, round and icy) and large (>1500 km, like small planets). The big regular moons formed in disks around the young jovian planets, like mini solar systems. Most are made of ices plus rock.

Orbital resonance (Io : Europa : Ganymede periods ≈ 1 : 2 : 4) means the moons tug each other at the same points again and again, keeping orbits elliptical. The varying distance to Jupiter means varying tidal stretch, and flexing generates heat, like bending a paper clip.

Titan (Saturn) has a thick nitrogen atmosphere (1.5× Earth's pressure) with methane clouds, methane rain, rivers and lakes: the only other world with erosion by liquid. The Huygens probe landed in 2005, and the Dragonfly rotorcraft is headed there. Enceladus, only 500 km across, sprays water geysers from its south pole, a subsurface ocean with organics. Triton (Neptune) orbits backward, so it must have been captured; it has nitrogen geysers.

Key insight · why small icy moons are more active than rocky worlds of the same size
(1) Ices melt and deform at much lower temperatures than rock, so modest heat drives icy volcanism (“cryovolcanism”) and tectonics. (2) Many have an extra heat source that terrestrial planets lack: tidal heating. The size rule from Ch. 7 still holds for rock; ice and tides change the game.

8.3 Rings

All four jovian planets have rings, and Saturn's are spectacular: countless icy particles (dust to house-sized), each orbiting Saturn like a tiny moon, following Kepler's laws (inner ones faster). The system is 270,000 km wide but only tens of meters thick. Gaps are carved by orbital resonances with moons (the Cassini division lies at a 2:1 resonance with Mimas), and shepherd moons (Prometheus and Pandora) confine narrow rings.

Origin: rings lie close to their planets, where tidal forces prevent particles from assembling into a moon. Particles collide and are ground down, so rings can't last billions of years unchanged. They must be replenished by impacts on small moons, or by the break-up of moons. Cassini data suggest Saturn's bright rings may be geologically young (a few hundred million years), which is still debated.

Myth vs. reality
✗ Jovian planets glow from fusion. ✓ Their extra heat is from slow contraction and differentiation. Fusion needs ~80× Jupiter's mass (Ch. 13).
✗ Saturn's rings are solid sheets, as old as Saturn. ✓ They're swarms of independent particles, continuously ground down and resupplied.

Practice Exam-style questions with worked answers

Quick QuizWhy does Neptune look blue and Jupiter reddish?
Methane in Neptune's atmosphere absorbs red light, so the reflected sunlight is blue. It's not about temperature.
Quick QuizWhy is Jupiter denser than Saturn?
Its higher mass and gravity compress its interior. The composition is similar (H/He).
Quick QuizSome jovian planets emit more energy than they receive because of…?
Ongoing contraction or differentiation (gravitational potential energy becomes heat). Not fusion or tides.
Quick QuizWhy is Io more volcanically active than our Moon?
It has a different internal heat source: tidal heating from its resonance-driven elliptical orbit. It isn't bigger.
Quick QuizWhat's unusual about Triton? Which moon has rain and erosion?
Triton orbits backward (captured). Titan has methane rain, rivers and lakes.
Quick QuizSaturn's moons shape its rings through…? And are the rings unchanged since formation?
Orbital resonances (gaps, shepherding). No: rings are continually resupplied by impacts on small moons.
ProblemIo loses 1000 kg/s of SO₂. Fraction of Io's mass (8.9×1022 kg) lost in 4.5 Gyr?
1000 × 4.5×109 × 3.15×107 ≈ 1.4×1020 kg, which is 0.16% of Io. If SO₂ were 1% of Io, it would last 8.9×1020/1000 s ≈ 2.8×1010 yr.
ProblemRings 270,000 km wide and 50 m thick, shrunk to 6.6 cm across: how thick?
50 m / 2.7×108 m = 1.9×10−7, times 6.6 cm ≈ 1.2×10−6 cm, ~8000× thinner than a dollar bill (0.01 cm).
ProblemPrometheus (139,350 km) vs. Pandora (141,700 km) around Saturn (5.7×1026 kg): periods?
p = 2π√(a³/GM): 14.7 h vs 15.1 h. Distances differ by 1.7%, periods by 2.5% (p ∝ a3/2). Prometheus laps Pandora every ~40 orbits, about every 25 days.

The shortcut: in Earth units, ρ = 5.5 g/cm³ × M/R³ (the 4π/3 and G cancel). Jupiter: 318/11.2³ = 0.23 → 1.2 g/cm³.
Earth's interior: surface rocks are 2–3 g/cm³, but the average is 5.5, so the deep interior must be much denser (the iron core, ~10–13 g/cm³).
The pattern: small, dense, rocky planets near the Sun; big, low-density gas and ice giants far out. Pluto fits neither group: it's a Kuiper-belt ice-rock world (Ch. 9).
Going deeper · which gases can a planet keep? And why rings? (thermal speeds, Owocki §15)
Gas molecules move at vth = √(3kT/m). A planet keeps a gas for billions of years if its escape speed is ≳ 6 vth. Jupiter (vesc 60 km/s): hydrogen at 165 K moves 2 km/s, ratio 30, kept. Earth (11.2 km/s; upper-air 1000 K): hydrogen 5 km/s (ratio 2, lost), nitrogen 0.9 km/s (ratio 12, kept). Moon (2.4 km/s): even N2 escapes. Mass and cold make giants; small warm worlds lose light gases. Rings: inside the Roche limit (~2.2 planet radii for icy moonlets around Saturn) the planet's tidal stretch beats a moonlet's self-gravity, so debris can't gather into a moon. Saturn's main rings lie inside it.
CosmosJupiter is 11.2 Earth diameters across. How many Earths fit inside?
Volume ∝ R³: 11.2³ ≈ 1400 Earths (but only 318 Earth masses: much less dense).
CosmosNeptune was found in 1846 from Uranus's wobbles; its period is 165 yr. When did it first complete an orbit since discovery?
1846 + 165 = 2011. (Galileo sketched it in 1613 without realizing it was a planet.)
OwockiTitan (vesc 2.6 km/s, 94 K) has a thick N2 atmosphere; our Moon doesn't. Why?
Cold: N2 at 94 K moves only ~0.3 km/s (ratio ~9, kept). On the sunlit Moon (~390 K) it's 0.6 km/s, ratio 4: lost.

30-second
recap
Jovian planets are H/He giants (Jupiter, Saturn) or ice giants (Uranus, Neptune), all around ~10-Earth-mass cores, with no surfaces, metallic-hydrogen interiors and powerful magnetospheres. Gravity compresses them, so more mass doesn't mean much bigger. Their moons formed in mini-disks and are icy. Tidal heating via orbital resonance makes Io volcanic and keeps Europa's ocean liquid; Titan has methane weather and Enceladus has geysers. Rings are swarms of particles, sculpted by resonances and continually resupplied.
