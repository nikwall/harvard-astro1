A Universe of Galaxies

Big question: What kinds of galaxies exist, how far away are they, and how did they grow up?

16.1 Islands of stars

  • Spiral galaxies: a disk with spiral arms, a bulge and a halo, like the Milky Way. Lots of cool gas and dust, ongoing star formation, blue-white arms. Many have a bar (ours does). Lenticular (S0) galaxies have a disk but no arms and little gas.
  • Elliptical galaxies: round or elongated, redder (old stars only), with little cool gas and dust, so little new star formation. They range from giants (the biggest galaxies, often at the centers of clusters) to the very common dwarf ellipticals.
  • Irregular galaxies: neither disk nor round, often small and gas-rich, e.g., the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds.

On a color–luminosity diagram (the galaxy version of an H–R diagram), star-forming spirals and irregulars form a “blue cloud” and non-star-forming ellipticals a “red sequence”. Galaxies live in groups (our Local Group: the Milky Way, Andromeda, M33 and dozens of dwarfs) and clusters of hundreds to thousands (Virgo, Coma). Ellipticals are far more common in dense clusters, a clue that collisions matter.

16.2 Measuring galaxy distances and Hubble's law

The distance chain: radar ranging gives the AU; parallax gives nearby stars (Gaia: thousands of ly); main-sequence fitting compares a cluster's main sequence with a nearby one; then standard candles, objects whose luminosity we know, so d = √(L/4πb). The two key ones:

  • Cepheid variables: pulsating giants whose period tells their luminosity (the period–luminosity relation found by Henrietta Leavitt at Harvard, 1912). In 1924 Hubble found Cepheids in Andromeda and showed it's a separate galaxy far outside ours, settling the 1920 “Great Debate”. They reach ~100 Mly.
  • White-dwarf supernovae: all nearly the same peak luminosity and visible across billions of light-years.

Hubble's law: using Slipher's redshifts and his own distances, Hubble (1929; Lemaître found it in 1927) showed that more distant galaxies move away faster: v = H₀ × d. This is the expanding universe: galaxies aren't flying through space; space itself is stretching, and light traveling through it gets stretched to longer wavelengths (cosmological redshift). Measure a redshift, divide by H₀, and you have a distance. That's how we map the universe beyond the ladder.

Age of the universe: if expansion had always run at today's rate, everything was together a time 1/H₀ ago, about 14 billion years. Looking far means looking back: a galaxy seen with a lookback time of 10 Gyr appears as it was when the universe was ~4 Gyr old. The cosmological horizon marks the limit where the lookback time equals the age of the universe; it's a boundary in time, not in space.

16.3 Galaxy evolution

Deep images are time machines: distant galaxies are seen young. JWST now sees galaxies from less than ~300 Myr after the Big Bang. Models start from a nearly uniform early universe in which some regions were slightly denser. Gravity (mostly of dark matter) pulled them together; gas collected in protogalactic clouds, cooled, and formed stars.

Why spirals vs. ellipticals? (1) Birth conditions: clouds with more angular momentum make disks; denser clouds convert gas to stars quickly, so stars form before a disk can settle, leaving an elliptical. (2) Collisions and mergers: galaxies are close together relative to their sizes, so they collide often. Two merging spirals can become an elliptical, which is why ellipticals dominate cluster centers. The Milky Way and Andromeda (approaching at ~110 km/s) will likely merge in ~5 Gyr. Starbursts (star formation 100× the Milky Way's rate, often triggered by collisions) drive supernova-powered galactic winds that blow gas into the halo, where it can cool and rain back: the star–gas–star cycle on a galactic scale.

16.4 Supermassive black holes and quasars

Active galactic nuclei (AGN) are galaxy centers that outshine their whole galaxy. The brightest are quasars, up to 1000× the Milky Way's luminosity. Some show radio jets hundreds of thousands of ly long. Quasars were most common ~10 Gyr ago: we see them only at great distances. Their brightness varies within days, so the emitting region is only light-days across, the size of a solar system. Only one engine fits: matter spiraling into a supermassive black hole through an accretion disk, converting gravitational potential energy into heat and light with ~10% efficiency, far more than fusion's 0.7%. Measured black-hole masses track their galaxies' bulge masses (~0.2%). Galaxies and black holes grow together, and AGN jets and winds heat halo gas and regulate star formation (“feedback”).

Practice Exam-style questions with worked answers

Quick QuizWhich galaxy do we see at its youngest: Local Group, 5 Gly away, or 10 Gly away? Center of a big cluster?
The one 10 Gly away (longest lookback). At a cluster's center: a giant elliptical (built by mergers).
Quick QuizHow do we get a Cepheid's distance? Best candle for very distant galaxies?
Its period → luminosity (period–luminosity relation), then the inverse square law. Farthest: white-dwarf supernovae.
Quick QuizWhy do nearly all galaxies recede from us?
Expansion makes every galaxy recede from nearly every other (raisin cake): no center, and we're not special.
Quick QuizLight that traveled 10 Gyr shows a galaxy when the universe was…? Key model assumption?
~4 Gyr old (14 − 10). Assumption: some regions were slightly denser than others.
Quick QuizA quasar's luminosity comes from a region the size of…? Its energy source?
The solar system (rapid variability). Its energy is gravitational potential energy of infalling matter, not chemical or nuclear. Bigger black holes go with more massive bulges.
ProblemM100 Cepheids: (L, b) = (3.9×1030 W, 9.3×10−19), (1.2×1030, 3.8×10−19), (2.5×1030, 8.7×10−19) W/m². Distance?
d = √(L/4πb): 5.8, 5.0, 4.8 ×1023 m = 61, 53, 51 Mly. Mean ≈ 55 ± 5 Mly (~10% uncertainty).
ProblemGalaxies recede at 15,000, 20,000 and 25,000 km/s (H₀ = 22 km/s/Mly). Distances?
d = v/H₀ ≈ 680, 910 and 1100 Mly.
ProblemYou (70 kg) fall into a quasar's disk, which radiates 10% of your mc². Energy? For a 100 W bulb?
E = 0.1 × 70 × (3×108)² ≈ 6×1017 J, enough to run a 100 W bulb for 6×1015 s ≈ 2×108 yr.
Myth check“What is the universe expanding into?”
Nothing: space itself expands, like the surface of an inflating balloon. The Big Bang filled all of space; it wasn't an explosion into pre-existing emptiness.

Going further History, deeper physics & more practice

History & method · from “spiral nebulae” to the expanding universe
1920, the Great Debate: Shapley argued the spiral nebulae were gas clouds inside a huge Milky Way; Curtis argued they were separate “island universes”. 1923–25: Hubble found Cepheids in Andromeda with the 100-inch telescope; Leavitt's period–luminosity law (Harvard, 1912) gave their luminosity, the inverse-square law their distance: far beyond the Milky Way. 1929: with Milton Humason's redshifts (Humason started at Mt. Wilson as a mule driver), Hubble found v ∝ d; Lemaître had published the relation in 1927. Hubble's constant came out ~500 km/s/Mpc, 7× today's ~70, partly because there are two kinds of Cepheids: the ones he used are ~4× brighter than he assumed, so every distance was ~2× too small (Baade, 1952). The universe instantly doubled in size and age.
Going deeper · distance tricks and quasar engines (Owocki §27–28)
Tully–Fisher: a spiral's luminosity scales as its rotation speed to the ~4th power (L ∝ V4), so a Doppler line width gives L and thus distance, beyond Cepheid range. Quasars: 3C 273 has z = 0.158, so v = 0.158c = 47,000 km/s and d = v/H0 ≈ 680 Mpc (2.2 Gly); to look as bright as it does it must emit ~1012 L☉, more than its whole host galaxy. It varies within days, so the source is at most a few light-days across. Accretion explains it: L = ηṀc² gives ~1012 L☉ for ~1 M☉/yr at η = 0.1. Lyman-α forest: each intergalactic hydrogen cloud along the way absorbs at 121.6 nm × (1 + zcloud), leaving a “forest” of lines that maps gas we can't otherwise see. Superluminal jets: blobs moving at >0.7c nearly toward us appear to move faster than light (a light-travel-time effect).
CosmosA quasar has z = 3.6. At what wavelength do you observe its Lyman-α line (121.6 nm)? Which band?
λ = 121.6 × (1 + 3.6) = 559 nm: shifted from the UV into visible green light.
CosmosA galaxy's Hα (656.3 nm) is seen at 670.7 nm. Redshift and distance (H0 = 70 km/s/Mpc)?
z = 14.4/656.3 = 0.022; v = 6600 km/s; d = v/H0 ≈ 94 Mpc (~300 Mly).
OwockiWhy do galaxies collide often but stars inside them almost never?
Stars are separated by ~107 of their own diameters; galaxies by only ~20 of theirs. Galaxies' gas clouds do collide, triggering starbursts.

30-second
recap
Galaxies are spiral (disks, gas, star formation), elliptical (red, gas-poor, common in clusters) or irregular. Distances climb a ladder: radar → parallax → main-sequence fitting → Cepheids (Leavitt) → white-dwarf supernovae → Hubble's law v = H₀d, which reveals expanding space and an age of ~1/H₀ ≈ 14 Gyr. Galaxies grew from slightly denser regions; collisions turn spirals into ellipticals. Quasars are accreting supermassive black holes, most common long ago, that co-evolve with their galaxies.
