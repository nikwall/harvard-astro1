10.1 Detecting planets around other stars

The problem: a planet is billions of times fainter than its star and lost in the glare. Seen from far away, the Sun would outshine Earth ~1010 to 1. So we mostly use indirect methods, watching the star rather than the planet:

  • Gravitational tug. Star and planet both orbit their common center of mass, so the star wobbles. (1) Astrometric method: measure the tiny side-to-side shift in the star's position (now done by ESA's Gaia). (2) Doppler (radial-velocity) method: measure the periodic red/blueshift of the star's spectral lines. It gives the orbital period (→ distance via Kepler 3) and the planet's mass. Because we see only the line-of-sight component, we get a minimum mass unless we know the tilt.
  • Transits: if the orbit is edge-on, the planet crosses the star and dims it slightly. The depth gives the planet's size; repeated transits give the period. Kepler (2009–18) and TESS (since 2018) found thousands this way. During the eclipse (planet behind the star), the tiny drop in infrared light gives the planet's temperature.
  • Direct imaging (block the starlight with a coronagraph; best for young, hot giants far from their stars) and gravitational microlensing (a planet's gravity briefly magnifies a background star).

10.2 The nature of planets around other stars

What we can measure: every method gives the orbital period and distance. Doppler and astrometry give mass; transits give size. Transit + Doppler together give density, and so a planet's composition type (rock, water world, gas giant). During transits, starlight filtered through a planet's atmosphere reveals molecules (transmission spectroscopy): JWST has measured CO₂, water, methane and sulfur dioxide in exoplanet atmospheres.

The big surprise: variety.

  • Hot Jupiters: giant planets orbiting in days, far closer than Mercury (51 Peg b: 4.2 days, 0.05 AU, ~1000°C). Some are puffed up by the heat.
  • Many orbits are eccentric; some are even tilted or backward relative to the star's spin.
  • Super-Earths (1–1.6 Earth radii, rocky) and sub-Neptunes, the most common kinds of planet, have no solar-system equivalent. Also water worlds and lava worlds.
  • Compact multi-planet systems: TRAPPIST-1 has seven Earth-size planets packed inside Mercury-like distances around a cool red dwarf, several in its habitable zone.

10.3 Do we need to revise the nebular theory?

The basic theory survives: disks around young stars are seen everywhere, and planets orbit in the same direction as their stars spin. But hot Jupiters can't have formed so close (too hot for ices, too little material), so we added planetary migration: while the gas disk is still present, waves in the disk exchange angular momentum with a young planet and it spirals inward (or outward). Close gravitational encounters between planets can fling them onto eccentric orbits or eject them (“rogue planets”). The theory needed modification, not replacement, which is how science normally works. Our own system's layout may be somewhat unusual, but Earth-size planets in habitable zones appear common.

Practice Exam-style questions with worked answers

Quick QuizWhich method could detect a planet whose orbit is face-on to us?
Astrometric: the star's side-to-side wobble is in the sky plane. Doppler needs line-of-sight motion; transits need edge-on orbits.
Quick QuizWhich methods measure mass? Which gives size? Which pair gives density?
Mass: astrometric and Doppler. Size: transit. Density: transit + Doppler together.
Quick QuizWhat is a “super-Earth”?
A planet similar in composition to Earth but larger (up to ~1.6 R⊕, a few M⊕). Not simply a hotter Earth.
Quick QuizBest explanation for hot Jupiters? When does migration happen?
They formed farther out and migrated inward, early, while the gas disk still existed. It wasn't the star's gravity pulling them in.
Visual SkillsIf the planet were more massive (same orbit), how would the star's velocity curve change?
Bigger peaks and valleys (a stronger tug); the period stays the same, since it depends on distance and the star's mass.
ProblemWhat fraction of the Sun's light does Earth intercept, and reflect (29%)?
πR⊕²/(4πa²) = (6.4×106)²/(4 × (1.5×1011)²) ≈ 4.5×10−10; reflected ≈ 1.3×10−10. Jupiter: ~2×10−9 (its size wins over its distance).
ProblemTrES-1 blocks 2% of a 0.85 R☉ star. Radius?
0.85 × 696,000 km × √0.02 ≈ 8.4×104 km (≈ 1.2 RJ).
Problem…and its mass is 0.75 MJ (1.4×1030 g). Density? Type?
V = 4/3 π (8.4×109 cm)³ ≈ 2.5×1030 cm³, so ρ ≈ 0.6 g/cm³: less dense than Saturn, a jovian planet.
Problem51 Peg b: period 4.23 days around a Sun-mass star. Distance?
p = 4.23/365 = 0.0116 yr, so a = p2/3 ≈ 0.051 AU, about 1/8 of Mercury's distance. A hot Jupiter.

Centre of mass: Mstar rstar = Mpl rpl; both circle it with the same period, and vstar = 2πrstar/P.
  1. Our Sun from afar (Act. 26): Jupiter (318 M⊕ at 7.8×108 km) moves the Sun 7.4×105 km from the centre of mass, just outside the Sun's surface, at 12 m/s. Earth moves it only 450 km, at 0.09 m/s. HARPS (0.5 m/s) would find Jupiter, not Earth. A low-mass star wobbles more, so Earth analogs are easier around red dwarfs.
  2. 51 Pegasi b: v = 56 m/s, P = 4.23 d = 3.65×105 s. Star's orbit rstar = vP/2π = 3.3×103 km; planet's orbit from Kepler 3: 7.7×106 km = 0.05 AU (8× closer than Mercury); Mpl = Mstar rstar/rpl = 333,000 × 3.3×103/7.7×106 ≈ 140 M⊕ = 0.44 MJ. It's a hot Jupiter (a minimum mass if the orbit is tilted).
  3. Transits (Act. 27): depth f = (Rpl/Rstar)²: Earth across the Sun = (6370/696,000)² = 8.4×10−5 (84 ppm); duration 2(R☉ + R⊕)/vorb = 2 × 7.0×105 km / 30 km/s ≈ 13 h, once a year; alignment odds ≈ R☉/1 AU ≈ 1 in 200, so surveys watch ~105 stars.
  4. Kepler-21b (Act. 28): P = 2.79 d around a 1.4 M☉, 1.9 R☉, 5.2 L☉ star. a = 0.043 AU; star speed 2 m/s → rstar = 77 km → M ≈ 5.5 M⊕; radius from the transit depth ≈ 1.6 R⊕, so ρ ≈ 5.5 × 5.5/1.6³ ≈ 7 g/cm³: a rocky super-Earth, at T ≈ 1850 K (a lava world).
Going deeper · the two detection formulas and their biases (Owocki §25)
Star's wobble: v ≈ 30 km/s × (mpl/Mstar) × (Mstar/M☉)1/3 (P/1 yr)−1/3, which favours massive planets on short orbits. Transit depth (Rpl/Rstar)², which favours big planets around small stars. The first finds were therefore hot Jupiters: an observational bias, not what's typical. The most common planets are super-Earths and sub-Neptunes. A transit spectrum (in-transit vs. out-of-transit) reads the planet's air (HW 3 #1c, Ch. 5); oxygen or methane would be possible biosignatures.
CosmosA planet has 10% of its star's diameter. How much does the transit dim the star?
(0.1)² = 1%, a Jupiter around a Sun-like star.
CosmosWhy do Doppler surveys give only a minimum planet mass?
They measure only the line-of-sight part of the star's motion: v sin i. A tilted orbit hides some of it, so the true mass is m = (m sin i)/sin i ≥ m sin i.

30-second
recap
We find exoplanets mostly indirectly: the star's wobble (astrometry, Doppler → period and minimum mass) and transits (dimming → size). Combined, they give density, and transmission spectra give atmospheres. Over 6,000 are known, with huge variety: hot Jupiters, eccentric orbits, super-Earths and sub-Neptunes. The nebular theory survives with one big addition: migration in the young gas disk (plus scattering). Planets turn out to be common.
