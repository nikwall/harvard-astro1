3.1–3.2 Ancient roots and Greek models

Everyone does trial-and-error thinking; science is the same thing done carefully and systematically. Ancient cultures were superb observers because the sky was their clock and calendar: sundials and Stonehenge-type alignments, lunar calendars (the Metonic cycle: 19 years ≈ 235 lunar months), Mayan predictions of Venus, Chinese records of supernovae and comets, Polynesian star navigation.

The Greeks invented the scientific model: a conceptual representation meant to explain and predict, judged by how well its predictions agree with observation. Their “Earth-centered” (geocentric) model had a spherical Earth at the center, surrounded by the celestial sphere. Aristotle (4th c. BCE) argued Earth is a sphere from the curved shadow in lunar eclipses. Aristarchus (3rd c. BCE) proposed a Sun-centered system, but it was rejected: no stellar parallax was seen, and a moving Earth “felt” impossible. Eratosthenes measured Earth's size (~240 BCE; see the Toolkit).

Ptolemy's model (c. 150 CE, the Almagest) put each planet on a small circle (epicycle) whose center rides a big circle around Earth. It predicted planetary positions well for ~1500 years. Islamic scholars preserved and improved Greek astronomy through the Middle Ages.

3.3 The Copernican Revolution

WhoWhenContributionLimitation or impact
Copernicus1543Sun-centered model; natural explanation of retrograde motion; computed planetary distances in AU and periodsKept perfect circles, so predictions were no better than Ptolemy's
Tycho Brahe1570s–1601Best naked-eye data ever (to ~1 arcmin); showed that a comet and the 1572 “new star” lay beyond the MoonCouldn't detect parallax, so he kept a hybrid Earth-centered model
Kepler1609, 1619Used Tycho's Mars data; abandoned circles for ellipses, giving the three lawsPredictions became 10× more accurate: the model now fit the data
Galileo1609–1632Experiments on motion (inertia) and telescope discoveries answered the three old objectionsTried by the Church in 1633; the Vatican formally acknowledged its error in 1992

Kepler's three laws of planetary motion:

  1. Each planet's orbit is an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. Eccentricity e measures how stretched it is (0 = circle).
  2. A planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times: it moves fastest at perihelion and slowest at aphelion. Earth is fastest in early January.
  3. \(p^2 = a^3\) (p in years, a in AU): more distant planets orbit more slowly, both in period and in speed.

Galileo vs. the three objections: (1) “A moving Earth would leave birds and clouds behind.” Answer: his experiments showed inertia, so objects keep moving along with Earth. (2) “The heavens are perfect.” Answer: he saw mountains on the Moon and sunspots. (3) “No parallax.” Answer: his telescope resolved the Milky Way into countless stars, suggesting they are far away. The clincher: Jupiter's four moons orbit something other than Earth, and Venus shows a full set of phases, which is possible only if Venus orbits the Sun.

Toolkit · Eratosthenes and Kepler 3
Eratosthenes (~240 BCE)$$\frac{7^\circ}{360^\circ}\times C = 5000\ \text{stadia}\;\Rightarrow\; C \approx 250{,}000\ \text{stadia} \approx 42{,}000\ \text{km}$$The Sun was overhead in Syene but 7° from the zenith in Alexandria on the solstice. The angle difference is the fraction of the full circle. The true value is 40,000 km.
Kepler's third law (Sun or a Sun-mass star)$$p^2 = a^3 \qquad [p]=\text{yr},\;[a]=\text{AU}$$Ellipse: perihelion = a(1 − e), aphelion = a(1 + e), and perihelion + aphelion = 2a.
Worked: Ceres at a = 2.77 AU: p = √(2.77³) = √21.3 = 4.6 yr. A planet with p = 3 months = 0.25 yr around a Sun-like star: a = (0.25²)1/3 = 0.40 AU, just outside Mercury's orbit.

3.4 The nature of science

Definitions
Hypothesis
A tentative, testable explanation (an “educated guess”).
Scientific theory
A powerful, simple model that explains a wide variety of observations with a few general principles and has survived repeated testing. It is not a guess, but it can never be proved true beyond all doubt.
Occam's razor
Among models that fit the data equally well, prefer the simplest.
Pseudoscience
Claims presented as science that don't follow its hallmarks (e.g., astrology fails controlled tests).

The three hallmarks of science:

  1. It seeks explanations for observed phenomena that rely solely on natural causes.
  2. It progresses by creating and testing models of nature that explain the observations as simply as possible.
  3. A model must make testable predictions that would force us to revise or abandon it if they failed.

The textbook “scientific method” (observe → hypothesize → predict → test → revise) is an idealization: Kepler's breakthrough came from years of trial and error, not a recipe. Science is self-correcting and must be verifiable: others must be able to repeat the observations. Newton's gravity wasn't “wrong”; it was incomplete. Einstein's relativity contains it as a limit and explains cases (like Mercury's orbit) that Newton's couldn't.

Myth vs. reality
✗ Columbus proved Earth is round. ✓ Scholars had known for ~2000 years, and even knew its size. Columbus badly underestimated the distance to Asia.
✗ Eggs balance only on the equinox. ✓ Nothing about gravity changes that day. Test it any day; that's science.
✗ Copernicus's model was more accurate. ✓ With perfect circles it wasn't. Kepler's ellipses made the leap.
✗ “It's just a theory.” ✓ In science, theory is the highest status an explanation reaches (gravity, evolution, the Big Bang).

Practice Exam-style questions with worked answers

Quick QuizIn the Greek geocentric model, retrograde motion occurs when…?
The planet actually goes backward on its epicycle around Earth. (The Sun-centered answer, Earth passing the planet, is the modern view.)
Quick QuizWhich was NOT an advantage of Copernicus's model?
Better predictions: it wasn't more accurate (circles). It did explain retrograde motion naturally and gave the planets' periods and distances.
Quick QuizEarth is closer to the Sun in January. By Kepler's 2nd law…?
Earth moves faster in January than in July. (Seasons are still from the tilt, not distance.)
Quick QuizA “highly eccentric orbit” means…?
It's much closer to the Sun in some parts than in others (a very elongated ellipse). It's not spiraling in.
Quick QuizTycho's and Galileo's contributions?
Tycho: precise data that let Kepler find the laws. Galileo: observations and experiments that removed the objections to a moving Earth. (Neither discovered gravity.)
Quick QuizEinstein's gravity showed Newton's theory was…?
Incomplete, not wrong. Newton's theory still works superbly except in extreme gravity or at high speeds.
ProblemPlanet “Nearth”: Sun overhead in Nyene, 1000 km north; in Alectown the Sun's altitude is 80°. Circumference?
Sun is 90° − 80° = 10° from the zenith, so the cities are 10° apart. C = (360/10) × 1000 km = 3.6×104 km.
ProblemEris orbits in 557 yr. Its average distance? Compare with Pluto (39.5 AU).
a = p2/3 = 5572/3 ≈ 68 AU, about 1.7× Pluto's distance.
ProblemHalley's comet: p = 76 yr, perihelion ≈ 0.60 AU (9×107 km). Its a and aphelion?
a = 762/3 ≈ 18 AU. Aphelion = 2a − perihelion ≈ 36 − 0.6 ≈ 35 AU (5.3×109 km), beyond Neptune.

Going further Homework, deeper physics & more practice

Homework · HW 1 #1 “Transit of Venus” (Hirshfeld Activity 2), worked
  1. Venus's orbit in AU. At greatest elongation (47°) the angle at Venus is 90°, so R = sin 47° × 1 AU = 0.73 AU (true value 0.723).
  2. Closest approach is at inferior conjunction (Sun, Venus, Earth in a line, Venus in the middle): 1 − 0.73 = 0.27 AU. That's exactly the geometry of a transit.
  3. Picture scale. Printed at 100%, the Sun is about 113 mm across; its angular diameter that day was 1890″, so the scale is 1890″/113 mm ≈ 17″/mm.
  4. Parallax angle. The two Venus images are about 1.7 mm apart (centre to centre), so θ ≈ 1.7 × 17 ≈ 28″ (1/67 of the Sun's diameter; the ratio doesn't depend on how big you print it).
  5. Earth–Venus distance with s = 7590 km: r = 206,265 × 7590 km / 28″ ≈ 5.6×107 km.
  6. The AU: r is 0.27 AU, so 1 AU = 5.6×107/0.27 ≈ 2.1×108 km, about +40% off the true 1.5×108 km. Your ruler reading may differ by 0.1–0.2 mm (±10%), so report two significant digits.
  7. Radar (1961): the pulse goes there and back, so r = c t/2 = 3.0×105 km/s × 276.2 s / 2 = 4.1×107 km; 1 AU = 4.1×107/0.27 = 1.5×108 km (+3%; with 0.277 AU it is exact).
  8. Why not radar the Sun? It has no solid surface: the pulse is absorbed and scattered in the hot, ionized corona at no definite depth, and the Sun's own powerful radio emission drowns the faint echo.
Why step 6 comes out 40% high. The photos were lined up on the Sun, but the Sun shifts too (by s/1 AU). You measured the difference of the two shifts: θ = s/rV − s/r☉ = (s/AU)(1/0.27 − 1). Solving, 1 AU = 2.7 × 206,265 × 7590 km/28″ ≈ 1.5×108 km, within a few percent. Halley saw this in 1716; the 1761 and 1769 expeditions (Cook sailed to Tahiti) gave the AU to a few percent.
Going deeper · the other ways to get the AU (Owocki §2.3; Hirshfeld Activity 1)
Radar at greatest elongation (Owocki Ex. 2-1): if the echo returns after 667 s, dEV = ct/2 = 1.0×108 km, and at greatest elongation dEV = cos 47° × 1 AU, so 1 AU = 1.0×108/0.68 = 1.5×108 km. Aristarchus (c. 250 BCE, Activity 1, see Ch. 2) timed the half moon: his method was right, but the 3° error in his angle made the Sun 20× too close. The lesson for exams: Kepler gives every distance in AU; one absolute measurement turns the whole solar system into kilometres. Transits of Venus come in pairs 8 years apart, once every ~120 years (Venus's orbit is tilted 3.4°): 1761/69, 1874/82, 2004/12, next 2117/2125.
CosmosPluto takes 248 years to orbit. Its average distance?
a = p2/3 = 2482/3 ≈ 39 AU (Kepler 3 with the Sun as the mass).
CosmosA planet orbits a 3 M☉ star at 3 AU. Period?
p² = a³/M = 27/3 = 9, so p = 3 years. Use ratios to Earth; no constants needed.
HirshfeldAristarchus used 87° for the Sun–Moon angle at half moon. How much farther is the Sun than the Moon? And with the true 89.85°?
d = r/cos E: cos 87° = 0.052 → 19×; cos 89.85° = 0.0026 → ~380×. A 3° error in the angle is a 20× error in distance.

30-second
recap
Science grew from careful ancient sky-watching. The Greeks invented testable models; Ptolemy's epicycles worked for 1500 years. Copernicus moved the Sun to the center, Tycho gathered precise data, Kepler found ellipses, equal areas and p² = a³, and Galileo knocked down the objections (Jupiter's moons, Venus's phases). Science = natural causes + simple models + falsifiable predictions. A theory is a well-tested explanatory framework, not a guess.
