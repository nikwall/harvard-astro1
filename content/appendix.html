Kepler · Newton · orbits (Ch. 3–4, 15)

\(p^2 = a^3\) [yr, AU; Sun-mass star] · \(M_1 + M_2 = a^3/p^2\) [M☉, AU, yr] $$p^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 a^3}{G(M_1+M_2)}\qquad M_r = \frac{r v^2}{G}$$ \(F = ma\) · \(F_g = GMm/d^2\) · \(v_{\text{circ}} = \sqrt{GM/r}\) · \(v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2GM/r}\)
\(E_k = \tfrac12 mv^2\) · \(L = mvr\) (conserved) · \(E = mc^2\) · tides \(a_{\text{tide}} = 2GMR/d^3\)
perihelion \(= a(1-e)\), aphelion \(= a(1+e)\)

\(c = \lambda f\) · \(E = hf = hc/\lambda\)
\(P/A = \sigma T^4\) · \(\lambda_{\max} = 2.9\times10^{6}\,\text{nm}/T[\text{K}]\)

Stars (Ch. 11–14)

\(b = \dfrac{L}{4\pi d^2}\) · \(d = \sqrt{L/4\pi b}\) · \(L = 4\pi R^2\sigma T^4\)
\(R/R_\odot = \sqrt{L/L_\odot}\,(5800\,\text{K}/T)^2\)
\(m_1 - m_2 = -2.5\log_{10}(b_1/b_2)\) · \(m - M = 5\log_{10}(d/10\,\text{pc})\)
main sequence: \(L \propto M^{3.5}\), \(t \approx 10^{10}\,\text{yr}\,(M/M_\odot)^{-2.5}\)

\(R_S = 2GM/c^2 \approx 3\,\text{km}\,(M/M_\odot)\) · WD limit 1.4 M☉ · NS limit ~2–3 M☉

The 12 big ideas of the course
  1. Scale: ~19 powers of ten from Earth to the edge of the observable universe, and looking far = looking back in time.
  2. The sky's motions come from Earth's rotation, orbit and 23.5° tilt, not from distance changes.
  3. Science works by testable models: Copernicus → Kepler → Newton → Einstein.
  4. One law of gravity rules everything, and orbits weigh things.
  5. Light carries composition (lines), temperature (σT⁴, Wien) and motion (Doppler).
  6. Planets form in spinning disks; the frost line sorts rocky from giant worlds.
  7. A planet's size sets its geology; its distance and atmosphere set its climate.
  8. Planets around other stars are common and varied, and planets migrate.
  9. Stars balance gravity against pressure; mass is destiny.
  10. Stars and their deaths forge the elements; galaxies recycle them into new stars and planets.
  11. The universe began hot and dense, is expanding, and is dominated by dark matter and dark energy.
  12. Life needs water, energy and nutrients, arose early on Earth, and the search elsewhere has begun.
Exam technique (from the syllabus)
  • Scientific notation, two significant digits.
  • Write the formula, then plug in with units, then compute. Show every step.
  • Sanity check: is the answer the right order of magnitude? (A planet heavier than its star? A speed above c?)
  • For “explain” questions, name the physical principle (conservation of angular momentum, inverse square law, …) and the evidence.
  • Ratios often avoid constants: e.g., brightness ∝ L/d², period ∝ a3/2, Schwarzschild radius ∝ M.
