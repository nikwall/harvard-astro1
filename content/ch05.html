5.1 Light and matter

Definitions
Wavelength λ · frequency f
Distance between wave crests; number of crests passing per second (Hz). For light, λ × f = c.
Photon
A particle (“piece”) of light with energy E = hf = hc/λ. Shorter λ means higher f and more energy.
Atom
A tiny nucleus (protons + neutrons, almost all the mass) surrounded by a much larger electron cloud. Atomic number = number of protons (which element); atomic mass number = protons + neutrons.
Isotope · ion · molecule
Same element with different numbers of neutrons (e.g., 12C, 14C); an atom with electrons added or removed; two or more atoms bound together.
Energy levels
Electrons in an atom can only have specific energies. Jumping between levels absorbs or emits a photon of exactly the energy difference.

Light is both a wave and a particle. As an electromagnetic wave it travels at c = 3×105 km/s. It also comes in photons, each carrying a fixed energy. From long to short wavelength (low to high energy): radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X rays, gamma rays. Radio waves are light, not sound, and “radiation” only means energy spreading out; it's dangerous only at high energies (UV and above).

Four ways matter meets light: emission (a light bulb), absorption (your hand warming in sunlight), transmission (glass, air) and reflection/scattering (a mirror, a movie screen). A sunflower is yellow because it reflects yellow light and absorbs the rest. The sky is blue because air molecules scatter blue light more than red.

Matter is mostly empty space: an atom is ~1012 times bigger in volume than its nucleus. Phases (solid, liquid, gas, plasma) depend on temperature and pressure; at the high temperatures in stars, atoms become ionized plasma.

5.2 Learning from light: spectra

Composition, from spectral lines. Each element, ion and molecule has a unique set of energy levels, so it emits or absorbs only at its own wavelengths: a fingerprint. Hot thin gas gives emission lines; cooler gas in front of a hot source gives absorption lines at the same wavelengths. Hydrogen's 3→2 jump gives red H-alpha at 656.3 nm; its 2→1 jump gives UV Lyman-alpha at 121.6 nm. In 1835 Comte said we'd never know what stars are made of. Spectroscopy proved him wrong, and in 1925 Cecilia Payne(-Gaposchkin), at Harvard, showed that stars are mostly hydrogen and helium.

Temperature, from the thermal (blackbody) spectrum. Any opaque object emits a continuous thermal spectrum that depends only on its temperature. Two rules: (1) hotter emits much more power per area (∝ T4), and (2) hotter peaks at shorter wavelength (bluer). Stars are nearly thermal emitters, and so are you (in the infrared, peak ~10 μm).

Motion, from the Doppler shift. Lines from a source moving away shift to longer wavelengths (redshift); moving toward us, to shorter (blueshift). The shift measures only the radial (line-of-sight) speed. Rotation broadens lines (one side approaches, the other recedes).

Toolkit · the laws of light
Wave & photon$$c = \lambda f,\qquad E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda},\quad h = 6.63\times10^{-34}\,\text{J s}$$
Stefan–Boltzmann law (power per m²)$$\frac{P}{A} = \sigma T^4,\quad \sigma = 5.7\times10^{-8}\,\frac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\text{K}^4}$$
Wien's law$$\lambda_{\max} \approx \frac{2{,}900{,}000}{T\,[\text{K}]}\ \text{nm}$$
Doppler shift (v ≪ c)$$\frac{v_{\text{rad}}}{c} = \frac{\lambda_{\text{shift}} - \lambda_{\text{rest}}}{\lambda_{\text{rest}}}$$Positive = away (red), negative = toward (blue).
Worked: at 10,000 K, P/A = 5.7×10−8 × 1016 = 5.7×108 W/m² and λmax = 290 nm (UV). Vega's Hα appears at 656.255 nm instead of 656.285 nm: v/c = −4.6×10−5, so it's moving toward us at 14 km/s.

5.3 Collecting light with telescopes

A telescope does two jobs, and magnification is not one of them:

  1. Light-collecting area ∝ D² (see fainter things). A 10 m telescope collects (10 m / 7 mm)² ≈ 2×106 times more light than your dark-adapted eye.
  2. Angular resolution (see finer detail): the diffraction limit θ ≈ 2.5×105 × λ/D arcseconds. Bigger D and shorter λ are sharper. The eye manages ~1′; Hubble (2.4 m) ~0.05″.

Designs: refractors (lenses; the largest is 1 m, Yerkes) vs. reflectors (mirrors; all big modern telescopes, up to 10 m Keck and the 25–39 m telescopes now being built). Instruments do imaging (cameras with CCD/CMOS detectors that count photons in pixels, often through filters), spectroscopy (splitting light by wavelength) and time monitoring (light curves).

The atmosphere's problems: light pollution; twinkling/turbulence, which blurs images to ~1″ (“seeing”); and absorption of most wavelengths. Fixes: mountaintop sites, adaptive optics (deformable mirrors correcting turbulence in real time), and space telescopes (Hubble, Chandra in X rays, JWST's 6.5 m mirror in the infrared). Space telescopes are not useful because they're “closer to the stars”. Interferometry links separate telescopes to get the resolution of one as big as their separation; the Event Horizon Telescope used an Earth-sized radio interferometer to image a black hole's shadow.

Myth vs. reality
✗ Magnification is what telescopes are for. ✓ Collecting area and resolution matter; over-magnifying just enlarges the blur.
✗ Stars twinkle by themselves. ✓ Turbulence in our air bends starlight. In space, stars don't twinkle.
✗ “Radiation” is always dangerous. ✓ Radio, visible and IR are harmless; only high-energy light (UV, X, γ) damages tissue.
✗ Tables are solid through and through. ✓ Atoms are nearly empty; solidity comes from electrical forces and quantum rules.

Practice Exam-style questions with worked answers

Quick QuizCompared to red light, blue light has higher frequency and…?
Higher energy and shorter wavelength (E = hf, λ = c/f).
Quick QuizThe set of lines in a star's spectrum depends mainly on its…?
Chemical composition (plus temperature, which sets which levels are populated). Each element has its own fingerprint.
Quick QuizA star's spectrum peaks in the infrared. It is…?
Cooler than the Sun (Wien: longer λmax means lower T). Size can't be read from the peak.
Quick QuizA line at 321 nm in the lab appears at 328 nm. The object is…?
Redshifted, moving away: v = (7/321) c ≈ 0.022c ≈ 6.5×103 km/s.
Quick QuizLight-collecting area of a 6 m vs. a 3 m telescope?
4×: area ∝ D², (6/3)² = 4.
Quick QuizWhy does Hubble beat most ground telescopes on resolution?
It's above the atmosphere (no turbulence). It's small (2.4 m) and not meaningfully closer to anything.
ProblemThermal emitters at 3000 K and 50,000 K: power per m² and peak λ?
3000 K: σT4 = 4.6×106 W/m², λmax ≈ 970 nm (IR). 50,000 K: 3.6×1011 W/m², λmax ≈ 58 nm (UV).
ProblemIf the Sun were 12,000 K instead of 6000 K?
24 = 16× more power per m², and the peak halves to ~250 nm (UV). Earth would be far hotter and UV-drenched: life as we know it is unlikely.
ProblemLyman-α (rest 121.6 nm) is seen at 120.5 nm and at 122.9 nm. Velocities?
(120.5−121.6)/121.6 × c ≈ −2.7×103 km/s (toward); (122.9−121.6)/121.6 × c ≈ +3.2×103 km/s (away).

Wavelengths: 5.0 eV → 248 nm; 6.9 eV → 180 nm; 7.2 eV → 172 nm (all ultraviolet); 1.9 eV (6.9→5.0) → 653 nm (red, visible); 2.2 eV (7.2→5.0) → 564 nm (yellow-green, visible); 0.3 eV (7.2→6.9) → 4.1 μm (infrared). With all four levels populated, the cloud shows six emission lines; only 564 and 653 nm are visible to the eye, the UV ones need a space telescope (the atmosphere blocks λ < 300 nm).
  3. Exoplanet test: compare the star's spectrum during and outside transits. Starlight crossing the planet's atmosphere loses photons at Pfohonium's absorption wavelengths, so the ratio (in/out) dips there: the transit is deeper at those λ (panel c). A cool atmosphere has its atoms in the ground state, so expect absorption at 172, 180 and 248 nm (the excited-state lines would be weak). Confirm: all lines at the predicted λ, with one common Doppler shift, repeated over several transits.
Going deeper · the Bohr atom, and why A stars have the strongest hydrogen lines (Owocki App. A–B)
Quantized angular momentum gives hydrogen levels En = −13.6 eV/n², so λ = 91.2 nm/(1/n² − 1/m²): Lyman (to n = 1, UV, Lyα 121.6 nm), Balmer (to n = 2, visible, Hα 656 nm), Paschen (to n = 3, IR). Balmer absorption needs atoms already in n = 2 (10.2 eV up; the Boltzmann factor e−ΔE/kT, with 1 eV ↔ 11,600 K). Cool stars have too few; above ~10,000 K hydrogen is mostly ionized (Saha). So Balmer lines peak in A stars: a temperature effect, not composition (Cecilia Payne, Harvard, 1925).

2.0 nm/mm, so 656.3 − 68 = 588 nm: sodium D.

30-second
recap
Light is an EM wave made of photons (E = hc/λ). Atoms have discrete energy levels, so each element emits or absorbs a unique set of spectral lines: that gives composition. Thermal spectra give temperature (σT4, Wien's law). Doppler shifts give radial velocity. Telescopes matter for collecting area (∝ D²) and resolution (∝ λ/D), not magnification. Space telescopes beat the atmosphere's blurring and absorption.
