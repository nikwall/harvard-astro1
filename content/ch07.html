7.1 Earth as a planet: interior, geology, atmosphere

Definitions
Core · mantle · crust
Layers sorted by density through differentiation (heavy metal sank when the planet was molten): a metal core, a rocky mantle and a thin low-density crust.
Lithosphere
The cool, rigid outer layer (crust + top of the mantle). Thin lithosphere means active geology; thick means dead.
Mantle convection
Hot rock rising and cool rock sinking, very slowly (cm/yr). It carries heat out and drives tectonics.
Magnetosphere
The magnetic field's “bubble” that deflects the solar wind. It needs a convecting molten metal core plus reasonably fast rotation.
Outgassing
Volcanic release of gases (H₂O, CO₂, N₂…), the source of terrestrial atmospheres and oceans.

Internal heat drives geology. Heat came from accretion and differentiation early on, and from radioactive decay to this day. Planets lose it by convection, conduction and radiation from the surface. The key variable is size: heat is stored in the volume (∝ r³) but leaks through the surface (∝ r²), so small worlds cool fast and their lithospheres thicken until volcanism and tectonics stop.

Four geological processes shape every surface:

  • Impact cratering: everywhere early on. Crater density measures surface age: few craters means a young, resurfaced surface.
  • Volcanism: molten rock erupting. It builds atmospheres and oceans by outgassing.
  • Tectonics: crust bending and cracking. On Earth this takes the form of plate tectonics (plates move ~cm/yr, with subduction and seafloor spreading).
  • Erosion: wind, water and ice wearing features down. It needs an atmosphere and weather.

What an atmosphere does: (1) absorbs dangerous radiation (X rays high up; UV in the ozone layer); (2) scatters sunlight (blue light scatters most, hence the blue sky and red sunsets); (3) creates wind and weather; (4) produces the greenhouse effect. Visible sunlight warms the ground, the ground emits infrared, and greenhouse gases (H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, but not N₂ or O₂) absorb and re-emit that IR, keeping the surface warmer than it would otherwise be.

7.2–7.4 A tour of the other terrestrial worlds

Moon & Mercury: geologically dead. Early volcanism flooded giant impact basins with lava, making the Moon's dark, smooth maria ~3–4 billion years ago. Mercury's long cliffs formed as its big iron core cooled and the planet shrank. Both are too small to keep internal heat, so they have had essentially no geology for billions of years, and craters pile up.

Mars: a victim of planetary freeze-drying. Mars has the solar system's largest volcano (Olympus Mons) and a canyon as long as the USA (Valles Marineris). Water once flowed: dry riverbeds, eroded craters, river deltas (Perseverance explores Jezero crater's delta), and minerals that form only in water. Rain stopped >3 billion years ago; water survives as polar and underground ice. Why it changed: Mars (half Earth's size) cooled, lost its magnetic field, and the solar wind stripped its atmosphere (measured by NASA's MAVEN). UV light split water molecules and the hydrogen escaped to space. Thin air means a weak greenhouse: cold and dry.

Venus: a hothouse world. Almost Earth's twin in size, so it's likely still volcanically active: its surface is only ~0.75 Gyr old by crater counts, but it shows no plate tectonics and no erosion. Its atmosphere is 90× Earth's pressure, 96% CO₂, and the surface is 470°C. Why: being closer to the Sun, Venus never kept liquid oceans, so outgassed CO₂ couldn't dissolve into water and get locked in rock as on Earth. It stayed in the air, a runaway greenhouse. Venus's water was split by UV and its hydrogen lost.

7.5 Earth as a living planet

Four features Earth depends on: (1) surface liquid water (moderate temperature); (2) atmospheric oxygen, made by photosynthetic life (not by volcanoes) and the source of the ozone shield; (3) plate tectonics, driven by internal heat; (4) climate stability from the CO₂ cycle (carbonate–silicate cycle): CO₂ dissolves in rain and oceans, gets locked into carbonate rock, is carried down by subduction and returned by volcanoes. When Earth warms, more CO₂ is pulled out, and vice versa: a thermostat acting over ~400,000 years. That's why most of Earth's CO₂ is locked in rocks, not in the air.

Human impact: burning fossil fuels has raised CO₂ from ~280 ppm (pre-industrial) to ~427 ppm (2025), and the global temperature is up ~1.3°C (2024 was the first calendar year above 1.5°C). The physics (Arrhenius, 1896) is simple: more greenhouse gas means more IR absorbed. Models and observations agree. Consequences include sea-level rise, ice loss and more extreme weather.

What makes a planet habitable? Size (enough internal heat for outgassing, plate tectonics and the CO₂ thermostat, plus a magnetic field) and distance (liquid water, neither Venus nor Mars).

Myth vs. reality
✗ Earth is full of molten lava. ✓ Mantle and crust are almost entirely solid; only the outer core is fully molten.
✗ The greenhouse effect is bad. ✓ Without it Earth would average −16°C. The problem is adding to it too fast.

Practice Exam-style questions with worked answers

Quick QuizWhich heat source still adds to Earth's internal heat today?
Radioactive decay. Accretion and differentiation heat were one-time deposits; sunlight only warms the surface.
Quick QuizWhich terrestrial planet has the thickest lithosphere? Which property matters most for geology?
A small one: it cools fastest. The key property is size, not distance or rotation.
Quick QuizA planet with few impact craters of any size means…?
Geological processes erased them (volcanism, tectonics, erosion). Everything was bombarded early on.
Quick QuizMost atmospheric gas? Strong greenhouse gas: N₂, CO₂ or O₂?
Venus (90 bar). CO₂: molecules that absorb IR are greenhouse gases; N₂ and O₂ aren't.
Quick QuizEarth's O₂ came from…? Where is most outgassed CO₂ now?
Life (photosynthesis). Most CO₂ is locked up in carbonate rocks; Venus's equivalent amount is in its air.
Quick QuizFlowing water on Mars: never, once, or today?
Important once, but no longer: riverbeds and water-made minerals, but no rain for >3 Gyr.
ProblemCompare the SA/V ratios of the Moon (r = 1738 km) and Mars (r = 3397 km).
Ratio = rMars/rMoon = 1.95, so the Moon loses heat ~2× faster. That's why Mars stayed active longer (Olympus Mons).
ProblemPlates move 1 cm/yr. How long until continents 3000 km apart collide?
3000 km = 3×108 cm, so t = 3×108 yr. Over Earth's history, continents have merged and split several times (supercontinents).
ProblemPlanet “Berth”: 8× Earth's mass, 2× its diameter. Density? Geology?
Volume 2³ = 8×, so the same density. Surface area is 4×, so SA/V is ½ of Earth's: it retains heat longer and stays geologically active longer.

Reading the table: Mercury and Mars match (thin or no air). Venus is predicted colder than Earth (its bright clouds reflect 75%) yet is 500 K hotter: a runaway greenhouse under 90 bar of CO2.
Going deeper · how thick is an atmosphere? (Owocki §15, 24)
Hydrostatic balance makes pressure fall exponentially with height, P = P0e−z/H, with scale height H = kT/(μ mp g). Earth (288 K, air μ = 29): H ≈ 8.4 km, so pressure halves about every 6 km and 99% of the air is below ~30 km, a skin 1/200 of Earth's radius. Mars (210 K, CO2, weak g): 11 km. Venus: 16 km. Titan: 20 km. Hirshfeld Activity 21 shows it to scale: with Earth's radius = 64 mm (1 mm = 100 km), the crust is 0.3 mm, Everest 0.09 mm, the deepest ocean 0.1 mm, the ISS 4 mm up. Owocki's three reasons Earth stayed habitable: a giant impact made the Moon (stabilizing our tilt); comets and icy asteroids delivered water; a molten-iron dynamo shields the air from the solar wind (Mars, without one, kept only 1% of Earth's surface pressure).
CosmosMercury spins 3 times for every 2 orbits (58.6 d spin, 88 d orbit). How long is its solar day?
1/Psolar = 1/58.6 − 1/88 → 176 days: two Mercury years from noon to noon.

OwockiWhy is Earth's air confined to a thin shell while the Moon has none?
H ≪ R for Earth (8 km vs. 6400 km), and molecules are slower than escape speed. The Moon's weak gravity (vesc 2.4 km/s) lets gas escape (Ch. 8's box).

30-second
recap
Size sets internal heat: big worlds (Earth, Venus) stay geologically alive, small ones (Moon, Mercury) die, and Mars is in between and froze. Four processes shape surfaces: cratering, volcanism, tectonics, erosion. Distance sets climate: Venus's CO₂ never dissolved into oceans (runaway greenhouse); Mars lost its air to the solar wind after its core cooled. Earth's water, oxygen, plate tectonics and CO₂ thermostat make it habitable, and we're now pushing CO₂ past 800,000-year highs.
