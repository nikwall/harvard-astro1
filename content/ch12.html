12.1 Measuring the properties of stars

Luminosity L is the total power a star emits (watts, or in L☉). Apparent brightness b is what arrives per m² at Earth. They're linked by the inverse square law: light spreads over a sphere of area 4πd², so b = L/(4πd²). To get L we need b (easy, via photometry) and the distance (hard).

Distance from stellar parallax: as Earth orbits, a nearby star shifts against distant background stars. Half the total shift is the parallax angle p, and d (in parsecs) = 1/p (in arcseconds); 1 pc = 3.26 ly. Even Proxima Centauri has p = 0.77″. Gaia measures parallaxes to ~0.02 milliarcseconds for 1.5 billion stars, revolutionizing the field.

Magnitudes (the astronomers' brightness scale, from Hipparchus): smaller is brighter, and 5 magnitudes = a factor of 100 in brightness. Apparent magnitude m is how bright a star looks; absolute magnitude M is how bright it would look from 10 pc, so M measures luminosity.

Temperature from color or spectrum. Hotter stars are bluer (Wien) and show different absorption lines. The spectral sequence OBAFGKM runs from hottest to coolest, with subclasses 0–9 (the Sun is G2). The different lines come from temperature, not composition: almost all stars are ~¾ H and ¼ He.

History & method · the Harvard “computers” (your Glass Universe reading)
The OBAFGKM system was built at Harvard College Observatory by women hired to analyze glass photographic plates: Williamina Fleming and Antonia Maury began the classifications, and Annie Jump Cannon ordered them by temperature and classified ~350,000 stars by hand. Henrietta Leavitt found the Cepheid period–luminosity law (Ch. 16), and Cecilia Payne showed in 1925 that stars are mostly hydrogen. The Friday section on the plate collection works with those same plates.

Mass from binary stars. Only orbits reveal mass. Visual binaries (both stars seen orbiting), eclipsing binaries (the orbit is edge-on and the stars block each other), and spectroscopic binaries (Doppler shifts reveal the orbit). From the period and separation, Newton's Kepler 3 gives the total mass. Star masses range from 0.08 M☉ (below that, no H fusion: brown dwarfs) to ~150 M☉. Radius follows from L and T via L = 4πR²σT⁴.

12.2 Patterns: the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram

Plot luminosity vs. surface temperature (temperature decreases to the right) and stars fall into groups. Size increases diagonally toward the upper right: at the same T, more luminous means bigger.

  • Main sequence (~90% of stars): all are fusing hydrogen into helium in their cores. Position along it is set by mass: massive stars sit at the upper left (hot, luminous), low-mass stars at the lower right. L ∝ M~3.5, so massive stars burn out fast: lifetime ≈ 1010 yr × (M/M☉)−2.5. A 10 M☉ star lives ~30 Myr; a 0.3 M☉ star, trillions of years.
  • Giants and supergiants (upper right: cool but hugely luminous, so enormous): stars near the end of their lives, done with core hydrogen and fusing other things at a furious rate. Betelgeuse's radius is ~4 AU.
  • White dwarfs (lower left: hot but dim, so Earth-sized): exposed cores of dead stars with no fusion, slowly cooling.
  • Luminosity classes: I supergiant, III giant, V main sequence (the Sun is G2 V). Some giants pulsate, like the Cepheid variables that are key distance indicators.

12.3 Star clusters and their ages

Open clusters (hundreds to thousands of stars, loose, young, in the galactic disk: the Pleiades) and globular clusters (up to a million tightly packed stars, very old, in the halo). All stars in a cluster formed at the same time and distance, making a cluster a perfect laboratory. Over time the most massive stars leave the main sequence first, so the upper main sequence erodes from the top. The main-sequence turnoff point sits at the stars whose lifetime equals the cluster's age. The Pleiades turn off at B stars (~100 Myr); globular clusters turn off below the Sun's mass, so they are ~12–13 billion years old, among the oldest things we know.

Toolkit · from observed to intrinsic properties
Inverse square law$$b = \frac{L}{4\pi d^2}$$Sun: L = 4π(1.5×1011 m)² × 1.36×10³ W/m² = 3.8×1026 W.
Parallax distance$$d\,[\text{pc}] = \frac{1}{p\,[\text{arcsec}]},\quad 1\,\text{pc} = 3.26\,\text{ly}$$α Cen: p = 0.742″, so d = 1.35 pc = 4.4 ly.
Radius from L and T$$L = 4\pi R^2 \sigma T^4 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{R}{R_\odot} = \sqrt{\frac{L}{L_\odot}}\left(\frac{5800\,\text{K}}{T}\right)^{2}$$Betelgeuse: L = 1.2×105 L☉, T = 3650 K, so R ≈ 850 R☉ ≈ 4 AU.
Magnitudes · binary masses$$m - M = 5\log_{10}\frac{d}{10\,\text{pc}},\qquad M_1 + M_2 = \frac{a[\text{AU}]^3}{p[\text{yr}]^2}\,M_\odot$$A 5-magnitude difference is a brightness factor of 100.

Practice Exam-style questions with worked answers

Quick QuizMove α Centauri 10× farther away. Its parallax…? What do we need for luminosity?
Parallax gets 10× smaller (p ∝ 1/d). Luminosity needs apparent brightness and distance.
Quick QuizTo get masses in an eclipsing binary you need…?
The time between eclipses (period) and the average separation, then Newton's Kepler 3.
Quick QuizCoolest: A, F or K? Most massive and longest-lived main-sequence star: A, G or M?
K is coolest. A is most massive; M lives longest (low mass → slow fuel use).
Quick QuizLargest radius: A supergiant, K giant or M supergiant? Hottest: B main-sequence, A supergiant or K giant?
The M supergiant (huge L at low T means a huge area). Hottest: the main-sequence B star (spectral type sets T).
Quick QuizClusters whose brightest main-sequence stars are white, or yellow, or with stars of all colors: which is youngest? Oldest?
Youngest: the one with stars of all colors, because it still has its blue O/B stars (a white turnoff means hundreds of Myr). Oldest: the one whose turnoff is at yellow stars.
Problemα Cen A: 4.4 ly, b = 2.7×10−8 W/m². L? How far must a 100 W bulb be to look as bright?
L = 4π(4.2×1016 m)² × 2.7×10−8 ≈ 5.9×1026 W (1.6 L☉). Bulb: d = √(100/(4π × 2.7×10−8)) ≈ 17 km.
ProblemA star with L = 8×1026 W has b = 3.5×10−12 W/m². Distance?
d = √(L/4πb) = √(8×1026/4.4×10−11) ≈ 4.3×1018 m = 4.3×1015 km ≈ 450 ly.
ProblemProcyon's parallax is 0.286″. Distance?
d = 1/0.286 = 3.5 pc = 11.4 ly.
ProblemSirius A: 26 L☉, 9400 K. Radius?
R = √26 × (5800/9400)² R☉ = 5.1 × 0.38 ≈ 1.9 R☉ (≈ 1.3×109 m).

  1. Photos 1 → 2 → 3: six months apart, so Earth is on opposite sides of its orbit for 1 and 2 (2 AU apart), and back at the same place for 3.
  2. The nearest star is the one that jumps between photos 1 and 2 and returns in 3 (just below-right of the top-left star in photo 1; it lands mid-frame in photo 2). Distant stars don't move: bigger shift = closer.
  3. Bessel's star: p = 0.00008° = 0.29″, so d = 1/0.29 = 3.5 pc = 7.2×105 AU. With 1 AU = 6 cm: 7.2×105 × 6 cm = 4.3×106 cm = 4.3×104 m = 43 km.
  4. To scale, Earth's orbit (6 cm across) and a star 43 km away: textbook parallax drawings exaggerate the angle enormously. The real angle is under 1″.
  5. Distances d = 1/p: Betelgeuse 150 pc, Bellatrix 78, Mintaka 210, Alnilam 590, Alnitak 230, Rigel 260, Saiph 200 pc.
  6. Grouped in space: Mintaka, Alnitak, Saiph and Rigel (~200–260 pc, members of the young Orion OB1 association). Not grouped: Bellatrix (78 pc) and Betelgeuse (150 pc) are much closer; Alnilam (~600 pc) is much farther, yet looks like a neighbour of Mintaka and Alnitak in the belt. Going deeper · magnitudes and the distance modulus (Owocki §3, 9–10)
m1 − m2 = 2.5 log(b2/b1); the distance modulus m − M = 5 log(d/10 pc), with M = 4.8 − 2.5 log(L/L☉). Sirius: m = −1.42, M = +1.40, so d = 101 + (m−M)/5 = 2.7 pc. Proper motion gives transverse speed Vt = 4.74 μ/p km/s (μ in ″/yr): Barnard's star, μ = 10.3″/yr, p = 0.55″ → 90 km/s. Binaries give L ∝ M3–3.5; spectroscopic binaries give masses only up to a factor sin³i.

30-second
recap
Parallax gives distance; distance + brightness give luminosity (inverse square law); color or spectrum gives temperature (OBAFGKM); binary orbits give mass; L and T give radius. On the H–R diagram, 90% of stars lie on the main sequence, a mass sequence of H-fusing stars (massive = hot, luminous, short-lived). Giants and supergiants are dying stars; white dwarfs are dead cores. Cluster turnoff points give ages up to ~13 Gyr.
